# Lesson 2.3: The Protocol — What the Auditor Reads

The [[protocol]] is the document that comes out of the exercise. Once the exercise is over, the auditor never sees the room or the people. The auditor sees the protocol.

A defensible protocol is worth more than a great unrecorded exercise.

## What the protocol must contain

Eight things, in order.

### 1. Date and time

The real-world date. The total real-time duration. The [[sim-time]] conversion (for example: "1 hour real-time = 8 hours sim-time"). This anchors the document on the calendar and makes the timeline readable.

### 2. Scenario summary

One paragraph that describes what the team rehearsed. Enough that a future reader knows the setup.

### 3. Signed participant register

Every person in the room. Their role for the exercise. Their signature or initials. The management body signature matters most. If it is missing, the auditor flags the protocol first.

### 4. Decision log

The full list of decisions, in order:

- The sim-time and real-time of the decision
- Who made it (named person, in their role)
- What the decision was
- Why they made it (their stated reason)
- What other options were considered, if relevant

The decision log is the core of the protocol. Without it, the document is just a date on a calendar.

### 5. Article 23 timeline

A short timeline that shows how the reporting chain was rehearsed: early warning sent at sim-time T+x, incident notification at T+y, intermediate report if asked, final report by T+30 days. This section maps the exercise back to Article 23 NIS 2.

### 6. Lessons learned

The findings from the [[hot wash]]. One or two sentences each.

### 7. Improvement items

Each item with the five fields from Lesson 2.2: what, owner, deadline, linked Article 21(2) and §30(2) BSIG references, done criterion. The improvement list is what makes the exercise produce a year of work, not just a day in a room.

### 8. Management body sign-off

A dated signature from the management body participant. This closes the Article 20(1) NIS 2 responsibility loop. The auditor reads this signature as proof that the responsible person was present and approved the findings.

## What auditors accept

- A dated PDF with a management body signature
- A structured decision log with names, times, and reasons
- Improvement items linked to article references with owners and deadlines
- Later evidence that the improvement items were closed (or evidence why they were deferred)

## What auditors reject

- A Word file with no signature
- A slide deck without a decision log
- "We did one but lost the notes"
- A protocol with no follow-up on the improvement items it produced

The last one is the most common defect. The exercise happened. The protocol was written. But nobody acted on the items. The auditor calls this theatre.

## The CIR connection

[[CIR 2024/2690]] Annex Section 3.6 requires that post-incident reviews "result in documented lessons learned to reduce the occurrence and consequences of future incidents." The protocol's lessons learned and improvement items meet this requirement for the simulated incident.

Annex Section 4.1 separately requires that the business continuity and disaster recovery plans be "tested, reviewed and, where appropriate, updated at planned intervals" and that the plans "incorporate lessons learnt from such tests." Your improvement items are how those lessons get into the plan.

## Key takeaways

1. The protocol has eight required parts: date, scenario, signed register, decision log, Article 23 timeline, lessons learned, improvement items, management body sign-off.
2. The management body signature closes the Article 20(1) NIS 2 responsibility loop.
3. CIR 2024/2690 Annex 3.6 requires documented lessons learned. The protocol meets this for the simulated incident.
4. CIR Annex 4.1 requires BCDR plan testing with lessons incorporated. The improvement items are how that happens.
5. Auditors accept signed PDFs with structured logs and follow-up evidence. They reject Word files without signatures and protocols with no follow-up.
6. The Live-System Tabletop module produces protocols in the same shape as real incident reports.
