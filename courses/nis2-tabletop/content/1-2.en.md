# Lesson 1.2: Who Must Be at the Tabletop

A tabletop with the wrong people produces evidence that fails the audit. The most common mistake is running the exercise with the IT team alone and giving the [[management body]] a written summary afterwards. That is not enough. The protocol shows the gap to anyone who reads it.

This lesson covers who must be at the tabletop, who is strongly recommended, and why the CEO or managing director cannot send a deputy.

## The five people you must have

### 1. A member of the management body

The CEO, a managing director, or someone with executive authority under company law. Article 20(1) NIS 2 makes the management body personally responsible. That responsibility cannot be met by reading a report later. The exercise must rehearse the decisions that only the management body can make in a real crisis. Things like:

- When to tell customers
- When to make a public statement
- When to engage outside lawyers
- When to involve the board

The minimum is one named person from the management body, present for the whole exercise.

### 2. A facilitator

The person who runs the exercise. They prepare the scenario, narrate each event, capture decisions, and keep time. They do not play a role in the scenario. They do not give the right answer mid-exercise. They stay neutral. Lesson 1.3 covers this role in more detail.

### 3. An IT manager or technical lead

The person who would receive the first technical alert in a real incident. In a small company this is the IT manager. In a larger one, the SOC lead or the on-call engineer.

### 4. A communications lead

The person who writes the customer message, the public statement, and any internal note to staff. Article 23(2) NIS 2 makes customer notification a duty in its own right. A weak message during a real incident creates more problems under data protection and consumer law.

### 5. A legal or compliance lead

The person who reads the regulator's questions, the cyber insurance policy, and any contracts that require you to notify customers or suppliers.

## Roles that are strongly recommended

You do not need these for every scenario. But missing them makes the exercise weaker:

- **[[DPO]] or data protection contact** when [[personal data]] is involved. Most incidents touch personal data, so the DPO is usually relevant.
- **External incident response provider** on the line, or represented by their phone number and a planned call.
- **Cyber insurance contact** represented the same way. Most policies have a notification window of six hours or less. The exercise rehearses hitting it.
- **HR lead** for insider scenarios.
- **Operations or production lead** for OT or physical scenarios.

## Why the management body must be there in person

Three reasons.

**The legal reason.** Article 20(1) NIS 2 is direct about personal responsibility. An exercise without the responsible person cannot show that they oversaw the response. The auditor reads a protocol without a management body signature as a defect.

**The operational reason.** The hardest decisions in a real incident are management body decisions: customer notification, public statement, board contact, outside counsel. Rehearsing these with someone other than the actual decision-maker teaches the wrong reflexes. When the real moment comes, the CEO has not practised it.

**The cultural reason.** When the CEO is in the room, the rest of the team takes the exercise seriously. When the CEO is absent, the team treats it as a compliance task.

## What if your team is small

Many mid-size companies do not have all these roles staffed. The realistic minimum is four people:

1. Management body representative
2. Facilitator
3. IT manager
4. Communications and legal lead (one person can cover both)

Four is enough to produce defensible evidence. Smaller than that and the exercise stops being useful.

## Key takeaways

1. Five roles must be at the tabletop: management body member, facilitator, IT manager, communications lead, legal or compliance lead.
2. The DPO, external IR provider, and cyber insurance contact are strongly recommended.
3. The management body must be present in person. Article 20(1) NIS 2 makes it a personal duty.
4. A protocol without a management body signature is read as evidence that the Article 20(1) duty is being skipped.
5. In a small company, four people is the realistic minimum.
