# Lesson 2.1: A Full Scenario: Ransomware on the ERP

This lesson walks through one full scenario. The exact details do not matter. What matters is the pattern. Every NIS 2 scenario follows the same shape. Once you can see the shape in one scenario, you can see it in any of them.

## The scenario

It is Tuesday, 09:14. Staff report that the ERP is not working. The IT manager checks and finds files with the extension `.lockbit3`. A ransom note appears on the operations director's laptop. The IT manager calls the CEO.

That moment, 09:14, is when the team becomes aware. The Article 23 clock starts now.

## Decision 1: Escalate to the management body now or later?

**Time: T+0 (09:14).**

The IT manager has two choices. Call the CEO right away, or first do an initial check and call after.

**Right answer:** Call now. The check and the escalation run in parallel. The CEO cannot make the cascade decisions if they hear about it two hours late.

**Wrong answer:** Wait for the check. Every hour without management body involvement is an hour the 24-hour clock keeps running without the responsible person knowing.

## Decision 2: External incident response: yes or no?

**Time: T+30 minutes.**

Engage an outside IR firm now, or try to handle it internally first?

**Right answer:** Engage now if you do not have a 24/7 internal IR team. A mid-size company almost never does. The IR firm should be under contract already. The phone number should be in the runbook. The hourly rate should be agreed in advance. The exercise tests whether the contact is current and whether anyone remembers the number.

**Wrong answer:** Call them late. An IR firm that arrives at hour eight finds an environment where evidence has been overwritten by panicked admins, files have been partially restored by curious users, and nobody can reconstruct the timeline.

## Decision 3: Cyber insurance: when?

**Time: T+4 hours.**

When does the insurance company get the call?

**Right answer:** Within the policy's notification window. Almost always under 24 hours. Often under six. The cyber policy is a contract. Missing the window can void your cover. The legal lead reads the policy and makes the call.

**Wrong answer:** Notify after the window. The insurer accepts the claim but reserves rights. Cover may be denied in part or in full because the notice was late.

## Decision 4: Which backup do we restore from?

**Time: T+4:30.**

The team needs to pick a backup point.

**Right answer:** The most recent backup that pre-dates the [[initial compromise]], confirmed clean by the forensics team. In ransomware cases, the attacker has often been in the environment for weeks before the encryption. The latest backup may already be infected. The right question is "when did the attacker first get in?" not "when was the latest backup taken?"

**Wrong answer:** Restore from the latest backup without forensic check. Two days later the encryption fires again because the malware came back with the restore.

## Decision 5: Early warning to the regulator: now or tomorrow morning?

**Time: T+22 hours.**

Article 23(4)(a) NIS 2 requires the [[early warning]] within 24 hours of becoming aware. The clock started at T+0 on Tuesday at 09:14. So the deadline is Wednesday at 09:14.

**Right answer:** Send it now (Tuesday 22:00). You are still inside the window with a margin. The early warning is short. You do not need full answers.

**Wrong answer:** Wait until Wednesday morning. The team plans to send it at 08:30 to be in time. Someone is late. The send happens at 09:30. You missed the deadline by 16 minutes. The auditor and the regulator both note this.

## Decision 6: The 72-hour notification

**Time: T+72 hours.**

What goes into the formal [[incident notification]]?

**Right answer:** An updated severity assessment, the known indicators of compromise, the impact on services, and the steps taken so far. The notification builds on the early warning.

**Wrong answer:** Copy-paste the early warning. The regulator reads this as inability to investigate, and the file ages badly.

## Decision 7: Tell the customers?

**Time: parallel to the 72-hour notification.**

Article 23(2) NIS 2 requires you to tell customers when the incident could affect them. §35 BSIG (Germany) is the parallel customer-notification provision.

**Right answer:** Draft a short, accurate, dated message. The communications lead writes. The management body signs it off. Send it without delay.

**Wrong answer:** Send nothing because the legal team is still drafting the perfect message. A short statement now is worth more than a polished statement two days later.

## Decision 8: The final report

**Time: T+30 days.**

What goes into the [[final report]] under Article 23(4)(d)?

A detailed description, the type of threat, the root cause, what was fixed, what is still being fixed, and any cross-border impact. The deadline is one month after the 72-hour notification.

## The same pattern in every scenario

The Live-System Tabletop library has twelve scenarios. CEO fraud phishing. Supplier breach. OT manipulation. DDoS. Water in the server room. Insider. Stolen executive phone. Compromised vendor patch. Each scenario uses a different trigger and stresses different Article 21(2) measures. The decision shape is the same:

Significance check → escalation → outside help → containment → regulator chain → customer message → final report.

Once you have run one tabletop, you can recognise the same shape in any of the others.

## Key takeaways

1. The clock starts when the team becomes aware, not when an alert fires.
2. Escalation, triage, and IR engagement run in parallel, not one after the other.
3. Cyber insurance has its own contractual notification window, separate from the regulator chain.
4. The latest backup is not the right backup in a ransomware case. Pick the most recent point that pre-dates the compromise.
5. The 24-hour, 72-hour, and one-month deadlines are fixed.
6. Article 23(2) NIS 2 customer notification runs on its own track.
7. Every NIS 2 scenario follows the same decision shape; only the trigger changes.
