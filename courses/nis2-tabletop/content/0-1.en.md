# Lesson 0.1: What a Tabletop Is and Why NIS 2 Demands One

This course teaches you how to run a NIS 2 [[tabletop exercise]] in your company. It is short. Fifty minutes total. Each lesson covers one part of the job: the law, who must be there, how to run it, and what to write down.

This first lesson answers two simple questions. What is a tabletop? And why does the law make you do one?

## What a tabletop is

A tabletop is a meeting. The people who would make real decisions in a real crisis sit around a table. Someone reads out a fake crisis: "Your company has just been hacked." The team then talks through what they would do. Who calls who. What gets decided. When.

Nobody touches a real system. Nobody calls the regulator for real. The clock is fake. But the decisions are real, and every decision is written down.

At the end you have a dated document. It lists the decisions, who made them, and what you learned. That document is the proof that you tested your incident response. An auditor will ask to see it.

## Why the law makes you do one

The NIS 2 Directive (EU) 2022/2555 lists ten cybersecurity measures every essential and important entity must put in place. They are in Article 21(2). Two of them anchor the tabletop:

- **Article 21(2)(b)** requires "incident handling." Your plan for what to do when something goes wrong.
- **Article 21(2)(c)** requires "business continuity ... and crisis management." Your plan for keeping the company running.

The directive does not say the word "exercise." But Article 21(1) says all measures must be "appropriate and proportionate." A plan you have never tested is neither appropriate nor proportionate. You have no proof it works.

CIR 2024/2690 (the Commission Implementing Regulation that gives technical detail for [[digital infrastructure entities]]) is more direct. Annex Section 4.1 requires the business continuity plan to be "tested, reviewed and, where appropriate, updated at planned intervals." Annex Section 3.6 requires post-incident reviews that produce "documented lessons learned."

Germany transposes these duties in §30(2) Nr. 2 and Nr. 3 BSIG. Other EU countries use the same words from the directive. Whichever country you work in, the duty is the same.

## What kind of tabletop

[[BSI Standard 200-4]] and the international standard ISO 22301:2019 Section 8.6 name four types of exercise:

1. **Awareness** — one person briefs the room. One-way. Cheap. Limited.
2. **Tabletop** — the team talks through a scenario. Two-way. Where most teams start.
3. **Functional** — the team uses parts of the real systems and tools. More effort.
4. **Full-scale** — the team runs the response close to real life. Highest effort.

Start with a tabletop. It produces the proof an auditor wants at the lowest cost. The other types are useful later, once tabletops have shown you the gaps.

## Why this is personal

Article 20(1) NIS 2 makes the [[management body]] (CEO, board, managing directors) personally responsible for these measures. EU countries must make the management body liable when the rules are broken. In Germany this is §38(2) BSIG.

The fines are large:

- **Essential entities**: up to €10 million or 2 percent of yearly worldwide turnover (Article 34(4) NIS 2)
- **Important entities**: up to €7 million or 1.4 percent (Article 34(5) NIS 2)

If your company has never run a tabletop, you cannot prove the incident plan works. That is the first gap an auditor finds. This course teaches you how to close it.

## Key takeaways

1. A tabletop is a meeting where the team talks through a fake crisis and writes down every decision.
2. The legal basis is Article 21(2)(b) and (c) NIS 2, plus the "appropriate and proportionate" rule in Article 21(1).
3. CIR 2024/2690 Annex 3.6 and 4.1 add detail for digital infrastructure entities. Other entities follow national law.
4. Germany transposes the duty in §30(2) Nr. 2 and Nr. 3 BSIG.
5. The management body is personally responsible under Article 20(1). Fines under Article 34(4)–(5) reach €10 million or 2 percent of turnover.
6. Start with a tabletop. It is cheap, fast, and produces the proof an auditor wants.
