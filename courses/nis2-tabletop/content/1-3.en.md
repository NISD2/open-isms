# Lesson 1.3: The Facilitator Cannot Also Play a Role

The most common mistake of a first tabletop is one person doing two jobs. The IT manager who narrates the scenario also plays "the IT manager being attacked." The CISO who wrote the scenario also makes the in-scenario decisions. When that happens, the exercise loses its neutral observer. The protocol that comes out is weaker.

This lesson explains why the [[facilitator]] must stay outside the scenario, what they do, and how a small team handles this.

## What the facilitator does

Four jobs:

- **Narrate the events.** Read each fake event out loud, or show it on the screen at the right moment. ("It is 09:14. Your IT manager calls and says the ERP is encrypted.")
- **Capture decisions.** Each time a participant decides something, write down who, what, when, and why.
- **Keep time.** Track real time and [[sim-time]]. Move the exercise forward at the planned pace.
- **Stay neutral.** Do not say which decision is right. Do not coach. Let the participants own their decisions.

## What the facilitator does not do

- **Does not play a role.** The facilitator is not the IT manager being attacked, not the CEO being woken up, not the regulator on the phone.
- **Does not coach.** If the participants make a poor decision, capture it and let the consequences play out. Coaching belongs in the [[hot wash]] after the exercise.
- **Does not advocate.** The facilitator has no opinion on any decision during the exercise.

## Why this matters for the evidence

The exercise [[protocol]] is what an auditor reads after the fact. The protocol has more weight when an independent person captured it. If the same name appears as facilitator and as in-scenario IT manager, the auditor has a fair question: who was watching the exercise?

The strongest protocol is signed by an external facilitator. The realistic standard is an internal facilitator who did not play in the scenario. The weakest is a facilitator who played roles. All three are legal. Only the first two are strong evidence.

## What to do if your team is small

Mid-size companies often have only one IT person, and that person is the only one qualified to write the scenario. Three ways to keep facilitator separation:

### Rotate

Change the facilitator from year to year. This year the IT manager facilitates while the CFO plays the in-scenario IT role. Next year the CFO facilitates while the IT manager plays in the scenario. Works when the team is small but cross-trained.

### Swap with a peer company

Ask another company you trust (an industry partner, a sister company, a friend in your industry association) to swap facilitators with you. Their CISO runs yours. Yours runs theirs. Both companies get a neutral observer at no cost.

### Hire an outside facilitator

Engage someone external for the day. Boutique consultants charge €5,000 to 25,000 for a session, depending on scope. For a first tabletop in a regulated company, the outside observer is worth the spend because the protocol becomes much stronger.

## Key takeaways

1. The facilitator never plays a role in the scenario.
2. The facilitator narrates, captures decisions, keeps time, and stays neutral.
3. The protocol's strength depends on the facilitator's independence.
4. A small team can use rotation, a peer swap, or an outside facilitator.
5. The Live-System Tabletop module covers the neutral-observer work that an internal facilitator would otherwise have to do.
