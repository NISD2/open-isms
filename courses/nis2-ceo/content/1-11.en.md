# Lesson 1.11: The Reporting Cascade and Significant Incidents

Five hundred thousand euros. Or five percent of your annual turnover, whichever is lower. If a cyber incident costs your company that much, you have twenty-four hours to pick up the phone and tell the regulator - and the clock does not wait for you to finish investigating.

## What counts as a significant incident

Article 23 of the NIS2 Directive requires covered companies to report [[significant incident|significant incidents]] to their national [[CSIRT]] or competent authority. The detailed thresholds are set by Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690.

Important on scope: the CIR's quantitative thresholds formally bind only eleven digital-infrastructure entity types (DNS providers, TLD name registries, cloud providers, data centres, CDN providers, managed service providers, managed security service providers, online marketplaces, search engines, social networks, trust services). For every other sector (energy, water, health, transport, public administration, manufacturing), "significant" stays qualitative, defined by national transposition law and the general criteria of Article 23(11) NIS2. In practice, those sectors also use the CIR numbers as the most concrete reference point available.

Article 3 of the implementing regulation defines an incident as significant if any one of the following is true: direct financial loss exceeds five hundred thousand euros or five percent of total annual turnover, whichever is lower. Trade secrets have been exfiltrated. A natural person has died or suffered considerable damage to health. Or there has been successful, suspectedly malicious unauthorised access that caused severe operational disruption.

## Recurring incidents: the Article 4 rule

Article 4 of the implementing regulation catches chains of small incidents. Two or more incidents with the same apparent [[root cause]], occurring within six months, that together cross the financial threshold are treated as one significant incident. A series of minor problems can become reportable retroactively. Track root causes, because the aggregation rule can trigger at any time.

## The three-stage cascade

The BSI sums up the logic in one phrase: "speed before completeness." You report what you know when you know it, not when the investigation is finished.

First: the [[early warning]]. Within twenty-four hours of becoming aware. What you know so far, not what you will eventually know. It does not have to be complete. It has to be on time.

Second: the [[incident notification]]. Within seventy-two hours. An updated assessment including severity, impact, and where available, [[indicators of compromise]].

Third: the [[final report]]. Within one month. Root cause, mitigation measures applied, and any [[cross-border impact]].

Late reporting is a separate [[sanctionable]] violation. If you skip the early warning because the situation is still unclear, you have committed two violations: the incident itself and the reporting failure.

Digital infrastructure providers (cloud, CDN, managed IT, DNS, trust services) face stricter availability-based triggers on top of the general thresholds - check CIR 2024/2690 Article 5 for sector-specific rules.

## What to build before you need it

Ask your incident response team: "Do we have a pre-drafted early warning template, and does the team know the CSIRT contact without having to look it up?" If your team spends six hours figuring out where to report, you have consumed a quarter of your window.

## Key takeaways

1. A significant incident is defined by CIR 2024/2690: five hundred thousand euros or five percent of turnover (whichever is lower), trade secret exfiltration, death or serious health damage, or malicious unauthorised access causing severe disruption.
2. Two or more incidents with the same root cause in six months that together cross the financial threshold count as one significant incident under Article 4.
3. The cascade is twenty-four hours (early warning), seventy-two hours (notification), one month (final report) - late reporting is its own separate fine.
