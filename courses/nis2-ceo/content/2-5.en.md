# Lesson 2.5: The CIA Triad and Business Impact

If your website were down for six hours today, what would it cost you in euros and in lost customers? If you cannot answer in numbers, your business impact analysis does not exist - and every NIS2 decision you make is based on instinct rather than evidence.

## What the rule means

Article 21(1) of the NIS2 Directive requires your measures to be proportionate to the "magnitude of societal and economic impact" an incident would cause. Underneath that phrase sits a three-letter vocabulary every security conversation uses: the [[CIA triad]]. Confidentiality - data is only accessible to the people supposed to see it; a loss is a leak. Integrity - data is accurate and untampered; a loss is corruption. Availability - systems are accessible when needed; a loss is downtime. Every security incident is classified as a hit to one, two, or all three.

The document that translates these categories into business numbers is called a [[business impact analysis (BIA)|business impact analysis]] - BIA. For each critical asset, the BIA answers: how fast must this be back up (the RTO and RPO covered in Lesson 2.8), and what happens to the business if it is not? The BIA is what makes the risk matrix from Lesson 2.1 actionable - without business-impact numbers attached to each asset, the scores are guesses.

## Why you care

Your role is not to calculate the BIA. Your role is to validate the assumptions in it. If the BIA says your order management system has a six-hour recovery target and you know your largest customer would leave after three, you correct it before it drives investment decisions. If the BIA rates confidentiality of customer data as "medium" and you know a leak would trigger customer notification under Article 36 and national press coverage, the rating is wrong - and the budget built on top of it is wrong.

The BIA is the bridge between the technical risk picture and your business reality. Only the management body has the business context to check whether the numbers match what the company can actually survive.

## What to ask your team

One question: "Show me the BIA for our five most critical systems. For each one, what is the recovery target, and does it match what our largest customers would actually tolerate?"

## Key takeaways

1. The CIA triad - confidentiality, integrity, availability - is the language every security conversation uses; learn to recognise which of the three is at stake in every incident your team reports.
2. The business impact analysis translates technical risk into business numbers - without it, the risk matrix scores are guesses.
3. Your job on the BIA is to validate the business assumptions, not to do the analysis - if the recovery time on paper is longer than the time your customers will tolerate, the document is wrong.
