# Lesson 3.8: Scenario - Ransomware Hits Friday Afternoon

On 19 March 2019, [[ransomware]] shut down forty Norsk Hydro production sites overnight. Cost: sixty million euros. Ransom paid: zero. In the first forty-eight hours, five decisions belong to the CEO alone - and those decisions, not the technical response, are what the regulator will examine.

## The legal framework behind the decisions

Article 23(1) NIS2 Directive: once you know you have been hit, you have twenty-four hours to file an [[early warning]], seventy-two hours for a formal report, one month for the final analysis.

Article 21(2)(b) requires an [[incident response plan]] before anything happens. Not the CISO's plan - the company's plan, which you approved.

The governing principle: "did the bad thing happen" and "did you do your job" are separate questions. The regulator answers the second by examining your paper trail.

## Walking through the Norsk Hydro case

Friday, 4 PM. Ransom note on every screen. Forty sites halted. LockerGoga ransomware. Cryptocurrency demand. You are the CEO.

**Decision one: declare the crisis.** Only you can activate crisis procedures. That flips the org chart for the duration. Wait until Monday and you waste sixty-four hours of [[containment]] - the twenty-four-hour regulatory clock ticks whether you declare or not. Norsk Hydro assembled the crisis team within hours.

**Decision two: authorise emergency spending.** [[Forensics]] vendors, external counsel, incident response firm - none are in the annual budget. Sign in the first two hours, or they arrive Monday.

**Decision three: contain before you restore.** Your instinct is to restore from backups. The correct move: preserve evidence first. Restoring before forensics examines compromised systems destroys the [[chain of evidence]] and leaves the entry vector open. Norsk Hydro shifted to manual production to buy forensics time - short-term cost, but it preserved the evidence that later cleared the CEO of personal liability.

**Decision four: the twenty-four-hour filing.** By Saturday afternoon, file the early warning with the BSI. File what you know, state what is uncertain, commit to a seventy-two-hour follow-up. Missing this window is its own violation.

**Decision five: do we pay?** The board asks within six hours. Correct answer: "not now, and only after legal review of [[sanctions law]]." A dedicated lesson covers the payment decision later. For the first forty-eight hours, payment stays off the table.

The response worked because the CEO was not improvising. Ransomware was on the risk register - rated very likely, very serious, with a board-approved response plan. Backups tested. Communication strategy pre-agreed. The CEO executed what the board had already signed.

The upstream failure: a phishing email compromised a single account months before the attack. Detection took weeks. Those gaps became audit findings - but did not create personal liability, because the incident response followed the board-approved plan.

## What this means for your company

Your incident response plan is the document that separates "the company was hit" from "the CEO failed." The regulator judges you on whether you followed a plan that existed before the attack.

Review yours this month. Confirm it names the five decisions above and has been signed by the management body. Without that signature, you are one Friday afternoon away from improvising - and improvisation is what regulators penalise.

## Key takeaways

1. File the early warning within twenty-four hours with whatever facts you have - do not wait for complete information.
2. Five decisions are yours alone: declare the crisis, authorise emergency spending, preserve evidence before restoring, file the report, hold the line on payment.
3. Whether you "did your job" depends on whether the response followed a plan your board signed in advance - not on how sophisticated the attack was.
