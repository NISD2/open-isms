# Lesson 3.3: When to Re-Sign-Off

Most CEOs treat the security [[sign-off]] as an annual chore. The regulator sees it differently. Certain events reset the clock, and if you do not re-sign when they happen, your last signature stops counting.

## The annual floor and the continuous duty

Article 20(1) of the NIS2 Directive requires management bodies to "approve the cybersecurity risk-management measures... and oversee its implementation." That second half - oversee its implementation - is the continuous duty most boards miss. Your sign-off is not a one-time act. It is a statement of fact that must be reviewed when the facts change.

BSI-Standard 200-1, section 7.4 hardcodes one specific obligation: a formal annual [[management review]] of the full security management system. This is the document the auditor reads to confirm you met your [[oversight]] duty. Annual is the floor, not the ceiling.

## The six events that reset the clock

Six categories of events require earlier re-sign-off. Any one triggers the duty on its own.

First: a change in leadership. New CEO, new board member, new managing director. The previous sign-off does not transfer. The new person reviews and signs in their own name.

Second: a significant change in the business. New product line, new sector, merger or acquisition, new geography. The risk picture changes, so the approved measures must be revisited.

Third: a major incident that exposed a gap. If something broke and the response plan failed, the measures need re-approval with that knowledge on the record.

Fourth: a regulatory change. New implementing regulation, new national law, new [[threshold]]. If the rules move, your approval moves with them.

Fifth: an audit [[finding]] requiring [[remediation]]. A finding signals something is missing. Fix it, re-sign, and create the paper trail the auditor expects next cycle.

Sixth: a material change in your [[supplier]] base. New critical vendor, key supplier going out of business, or a supplier incident that changes your exposure. Supply chain is a defined risk area under the law; your approval covers the suppliers those measures depend on.

## What re-sign-off looks like in practice

You are nine months into your annual [[cadence|cycle]]. Your CFO says the company is acquiring a competitor with eighty employees and a warehouse in Poland. You do not wait for the annual cycle. You put a re-sign-off on the calendar for the month after close.

First question to your CISO: "What systems does the target have, who are their suppliers, and what is the state of their risk analysis?" If the answer is "we do not know yet," the sign-off waits until the answer exists.

Second question: "Are we bringing their suppliers into our supply chain, or cutting them?" Their suppliers are now yours unless you actively remove them - and suppliers are in scope of the measures you signed off on.

Document three things: the [[trigger event]] ("acquisition closed on this date"), the changes to the measures, and the new signature. That paper trail turns a good decision into a defensible one. Skip it, and you have broken the continuous oversight duty under Article 20(1).

## Key takeaways

1. Annual is the floor, not the ceiling - six types of events force re-sign-off before the yearly cycle.
2. Change in leadership, change in the business, major incidents, regulatory changes, audit findings, and material supplier changes all trigger re-sign-off.
3. Every re-sign-off needs three things documented: the trigger, the changes since last approval, and the new signature.
