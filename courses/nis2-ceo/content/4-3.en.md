# Lesson 4.3: Cyber Insurance vs D&O - Different Products, Different Coverage

Most CEOs believe that if the company has [[cyber insurance]], they are personally covered for a cyber claim. The opposite is true. Cyber insurance protects the company; [[D&O insurance]] protects the director. They are different products covering different things - and the claim the regulator files under NIS2 lands in the gap between them.

## Two policies, two different named insureds

Article 32(5) of the NIS2 Directive lets regulators send the bill for serious violations to you personally, not only to the company. That personal claim has to be defended by a policy that names you personally as the [[named insured]].

As Lesson 4.2 covered, D&O insurance names the director as the insured - it was built for business judgment claims. Cyber insurance names the company. It pays for the costs of handling a cyber incident: [[forensics|forensic investigation]], incident response, customer notification, and [[business interruption]] losses. These are two separate pools of money for two separate types of loss. The company's cyber policy does not pay your personal legal bills. Your D&O policy does not pay the company's forensics vendor.

## The gap where personal liability lives

The dangerous scenario is when a director is sued personally for a cyber incident. The D&O policy often has an explicit cyber exclusion. The cyber policy explicitly does not cover individual directors. The director is uncovered. That gap is where the expensive surprise lives.

There is often a third product that completes the picture: a legal defence policy specifically for criminal or administrative proceedings against directors. Brokers sell it as a separate line item. It covers the defence lawyer, not the settlement or the fine. Whether you need it depends on your jurisdiction and your [[exposure]] - ask your broker when they next quote on D&O.

## What to demand from your broker

Your broker should be able to produce, on one page, a map of which claim scenarios are covered by which policy. A ransomware attack where the company suffers downtime - that is the cyber policy. A shareholder suing a director for failing to prevent the attack - that is the D&O policy, if the cyber exclusion does not apply. The regulator filing a personal [[sanction]] against a board member under Article 32(5) - that may fall in the gap between both.

Ask for the map in writing before the next renewal. If it shows a gap in the personal liability column, that gap sits on your personal balance sheet uninsured.

## Key takeaways

1. D&O insurance protects directors personally; cyber insurance protects the company - they are different products with different named insureds.
2. A cyber incident claim filed against you personally under Article 32(5) falls in the gap between the two - your D&O may exclude cyber, and your cyber policy does not name directors.
3. Your broker should produce a one-page map of which claim scenarios each policy covers - if they cannot, you are guessing at your own exposure.
