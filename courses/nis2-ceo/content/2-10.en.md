# Lesson 2.10: Measure 5 - Acquisition, Development, and Maintenance

The Equifax breach from Lesson 2.2 started because a known vulnerability went unpatched for two months on a server nobody knew existed. The patch was available. The team was capable. The process to apply it on time did not exist. Measure 5 is the discipline that closes that gap - not just for patching, but for every system your company buys, builds, or runs.

## What this measure means

Article 21(2)(e) of the NIS2 Directive requires "security in network and information systems acquisition, development and maintenance, including [[vulnerability handling]] and disclosure." Three duties in one clause.

Secure acquisition: when your company buys a new system, security requirements are part of the purchase decision, not an afterthought. The same contractual clauses from Lesson 2.9 (notification, audit rights, baselines) apply here.

Secure development: if your company writes software - even just an internal portal - it must follow a documented secure development standard. Your development lead should be able to name which one and show you where it is written down.

Vulnerability handling: the continuous piece. Known vulnerabilities are published every day. Your company must have a process that finds the ones that affect you, rates them by severity, and patches them within a written deadline. Your team calls those deadlines [[patch SLA|patch SLAs]]. The specific timelines are yours to set, but they must exist on paper and the results must be tracked.

There is a fourth piece in the same clause: [[responsible disclosure|vulnerability disclosure]]. If a security researcher finds a bug in your product, there must be a published way for them to report it to you. Some NIS2-covered entities are required to have a public disclosure page.

## What happens if you don't have it

Unpatched known vulnerabilities are the entry point in the majority of breaches. The auditor will ask for the patch SLA document and for the quarterly report showing whether the SLA was met. If neither exists, the finding writes itself - and if a breach follows an unpatched vulnerability, the question of whether you had a process becomes the question of whether your sign-off was informed.

## What to ask your team

Two questions. "What is our patch SLA on critical vulnerabilities, and in the last quarter, on what percentage of systems did we meet it?" If the answer is vague, the process does not exist. "Do our developers follow a documented secure development standard, and where is it written down?" If the answer is no, that is an action item.

## Key takeaways

1. Measure 5 bundles three duties - secure acquisition, secure development, and vulnerability handling - and the one that matters most month to month is patching, because unpatched known vulnerabilities are the most common entry point for attackers.
2. A written patch SLA and a quarterly report on whether you are meeting it is the single most important operational evidence for this measure.
3. Vulnerability disclosure - a published way for outsiders to report bugs to you - is part of the same clause and may require a public page depending on your entity type.
