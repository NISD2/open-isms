# Lesson 2.6: Measure 1 - Risk Analysis and Information Security Policies

Not one document, and not a fixed number either. The most common CEO mistake on Measure 1 is to hand the auditor "our security policy" as if one document settled it. The law asks for policies on risk analysis and on information system security, and how many documents that takes depends on who you are.

## What this measure means

Article 21(2)(a) of the NIS2 Directive names the first of the ten measures: "policies on risk analysis and information system security." That covers two things. First, a written method your company follows for identifying and scoring risks - the [[risk analysis methodology]] (Lesson 2.1 covered the equation behind it). Second, a top-level document that says how the company approaches security overall, with supporting policies underneath it. The top-level document is called the [[information security policy]]. The whole set of documents, people, and review cycles around them is what your CISO calls an [[ISMS]] - an Information Security Management System.

For most companies, Article 21(2)(a), and in Germany § 30(2) no. 1 BSIG, asks for these policies without a set number of documents or a fixed review interval. The CIR - the EU regulation that details Article 21 for certain digital providers (covered in Lesson 1.1) - goes further for the entity types in its Article 1: DNS, TLD, cloud, data centre, CDN, managed and managed security services, online marketplaces, search engines, social networks and trust services. They need the top-level policy, a risk management framework, and topic-specific policies on incident handling, supply chain security, security testing, effectiveness assessment, cryptography, access control (with privileged accounts as part of it), asset handling, and removable media. Security testing, privileged accounts, and removable media are easy to miss when starting from a generic ISO policy set, so they are a useful check even outside the CIR.

## What happens if you don't have it

The top-level policy is management's, not the IT department's or the compliance team's. Article 20(1) has the management body approve the cybersecurity measures, and the BSI expects the policy to be signed by top management and published in its name. CIR providers must also state the date of formal approval by the management body in the policy (CIR Annex 1.1.1(k)) and review it at least annually (Annex 1.1.2). An unsigned top-level policy is a fast path to an audit finding, because it means the management body either did not approve the security approach or cannot prove it did. Topic-specific policies can be approved by someone senior enough to decide - they do not all need your signature, but each needs an approval the auditor can verify.

## What to ask your team

Ask your team to produce one table (or on a grc platform etc): one row per policy you have, with columns for the document name, the approval date, and the approver. A blank in any row is a gap. Assign an owner and a deadline to every blank row, and put the assignments in the meeting minutes - that is the evidence trail.

## Key takeaways

1. Measure 1 is not one document, and outside the CIR there is no fixed number: Article 21(2)(a) asks for policies on risk analysis and information system security, approved by management.
2. CIR providers need the top-level policy plus the topic-specific policies the CIR Annex lists; security testing, privileged accounts, and removable media are the ones a generic ISO set most often misses - worth checking for anyone.
3. For CIR providers the top-level policy states the date of management approval and is reviewed at least annually; for everyone, an unsigned top-level policy is a fast audit finding.
