# Lesson 2.6: Measure 1 - Risk Analysis and Information Security Policies

Eleven policies. Not one. The most common CEO mistake on Measure 1 is to hand the auditor "our security policy" as if it were one document. The CIR - the EU regulation that implements NIS2 (covered in Lesson 1.1) - requires eleven distinct policies, and the top-level one has to carry your personal signature and your name on the published version.

## What this measure means

Article 21(2)(a) of the NIS2 Directive names the first of the ten measures: "policies on risk analysis and information system security." That covers two things. First, a written method your company follows for identifying and scoring risks - the [[risk analysis methodology]] (Lesson 2.1 covered the equation behind it). Second, a top-level document that says how the company approaches security overall, with supporting policies underneath it. The top-level document is called the [[information security policy]]. The whole set of documents, people, and review cycles around them is what your CISO calls an [[ISMS]] - an Information Security Management System.

The CIR Annex requires the top-level policy plus ten topic-specific policies: risk management, incident handling, supply chain security, security testing, effectiveness assessment, cryptography, access control, privileged accounts, information and asset handling, and removable media. Three of those cover niche topics that are easy to miss when starting from a generic ISO policy set: security testing, privileged accounts, and removable media - call them out and check them first.

## What happens if you don't have it

The top-level policy must carry your personal signature and be published in your name - not "the IT department" or "the compliance team." The CIR requires the top-level policy to carry the date of formal approval by the management body. An undated or unsigned top-level policy is the fastest path to an audit finding, because it means the management body either did not approve the security approach or cannot prove they did. The top-level policy must be reviewed annually. The ten topic-specific policies can be approved by someone senior enough to decide - they do not all need your signature, but every one needs a dated approval the auditor can verify.

## What to ask your team

Ask your team to produce one table (or on a grc platform etc): eleven rows, one per policy, with columns for the document name, the approval date, and the approver. A blank in any row is a gap. Assign an owner and a deadline to every blank row, and put the assignments in the meeting minutes - that is the evidence trail.

## Key takeaways

1. Measure 1 is not one policy - the CIR requires eleven distinct policies, and the top-level information security policy must carry your personal signature and name.
2. The CIR Annex explicitly requires three niche topic-specific policies that are easy to miss when adapting a generic ISO set: security testing, privileged accounts, and removable media - check those first.
3. The top-level policy must be reviewed annually with a dated management body approval; an undated or unsigned policy is the fastest audit finding.
