# Lesson 2.13: Measure 8 - Cryptography

A ransomware group gets into your network and spends two weeks quietly copying your data out. Then they lock your files and send the ransom note. On the day that note appears, one question decides whether you have a manageable incident or a front-page disaster: was the data they copied out already encrypted by you, or was it readable? You do not need to become a cryptographer. You need to ask four questions.

## What this measure means

Article 21(2)(h) of the NIS2 Directive requires "policies and procedures regarding the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption." The CIR (Annex 9.1) names the cryptography policy as one of the eleven required policies (Lesson 2.6).

Two concepts matter. [[Encryption at rest]] means data sitting on disk, in a database, or in a backup is scrambled so that someone who steals the storage cannot read it. [[Encryption in transit]] means data moving between systems is scrambled so that someone who intercepts the traffic cannot read it. Your company needs both, and the policy must name where each applies.

## What happens if you don't have it

The algorithms your team uses change over time - what was strong five years ago may be weak today. Rather than memorising algorithm names, the right reference for European companies is [[BSI TR-02102]], which BSI updates as research evolves. Your policy should reference TR-02102. (or similar) by name so it stays current automatically.

[[Key management]] decides whether the encryption is sound or hollow. If the encryption key is stored on the same server as the encrypted data, anyone who compromises the server gets both - that is not a control, it is a gap. Keys must be stored separately, access to them must be restricted and logged, and they must be rotated on a schedule. A perfect algorithm with a leaked key is the same as no encryption at all.

## What to ask your team

Four questions before you sign the cryptography policy. One: what is encrypted? The policy should map to your asset inventory (Lesson 2.2) - which assets hold data at rest and which transmit data in transit. Two: where are the keys? If the answer is "on the same server," that is a gap. Three: who can access the keys? Only specific roles, logged. Four: when were the keys last rotated?

Ideally your team maintains a cryptography policy that includes a list of approved algorithms and protocols the company allows - checked against BSI TR-02102 or an equivalent guideline. That list becomes the reference your IT team uses when choosing encryption for any new system or service. Sign the policy with a date and archive the previous version.

## Key takeaways

1. The cryptography policy must cover encryption at rest and encryption in transit, and it must name what is encrypted - "everything" is not an answer an auditor accepts.
2. Key management decides whether the encryption is sound: ask where the keys live, who can access them, and when they were last rotated.
3. Reference BSI TR-02102 in the policy rather than hardcoding algorithm names, so the policy stays current and the auditor has a named benchmark.
