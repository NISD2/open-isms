# Lesson 1.1: What NIS2 Is and Why It Exists

Roughly one hundred and sixty thousand companies across the European Union are now on a list the EU decided is too important to go dark. If your company is on that list, the rules governing your cybersecurity are no longer set by you - they are set by three layers of EU and national law, enforced by a national regulator who can [[audit]] your company and [[fine]] it, or find you [[personally responsible|personally responsible]].

## The directive

The formal name is [[Directive]] (EU) 2022/2555 - the [[NIS2 Directive]]. One EU-wide [[rulebook]] on how companies in [[critical sectors]] protect themselves against cyber attacks and what they must do when something goes wrong. It was adopted on 14 December 2022 and entered into force on 16 January 2023. It replaces NIS1, the EU's first attempt at a cross-border cybersecurity law, which was widely seen as inconsistent and weakly enforced - almost no fines were ever issued under NIS1 anywhere in the EU.

NIS2 is a directive, not a [[regulation]]. A regulation - like the GDPR - applies directly in every country. A directive tells each country what the result has to be, and each country writes its own national law to get there. Countries can be stricter than the directive, but not softer. The directive is the floor, not the ceiling.

## The implementing regulation (CIR)

On top of the directive sits a second piece of EU law: Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690 - the [[CIR]]. This IS a regulation, so it applies directly in all 27 member states without [[transposition]]. The CIR sets the detailed technical requirements: what counts as a significant incident, what policies you must have, what [[threshold|thresholds]] trigger mandatory reporting. These rules are the same everywhere. Lessons 1.11 and 2.5 cover the specific thresholds and the eleven mandatory policies the CIR requires.

## National law and the enforcer

Each country transposes the directive into its own national law. In Germany, that law is the [[BSIG]] (the Federal IT Security Act, amended by the NIS2 Implementation Act). The name changes by country; the duties do not.

Each country designates a [[competent authority]] - the regulator who supervises, audits, and fines covered companies. In Germany, the competent authority is the BSI (Federal Office for Information Security). The competent authority can conduct audits on its own initiative for essential entities (Lesson 1.2), require you to register (Lesson 1.4), require you to report incidents (Lesson 1.11), and impose fines up to ten million euros or two percent of global [[turnover]] (Lesson 1.3).

The full legal chain: **EU Directive (NIS2)** sets the goals -> **EU Regulation (CIR)** sets the detailed technical rules -> **National law (BSIG in Germany)** transposes the directive -> **National enforcer (BSI in Germany)** audits and fines you.

The first question to settle at your leadership table: does NIS2 apply to us? If your company operates in a covered sector and meets the size threshold, the answer is yes - and the obligations flow through all three layers to you personally. The next lesson walks through how that determination works.

## What NIS2 does not cover

NIS2 is about [[data security]] - the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of your network and information systems. It does not regulate whether you are allowed to collect, store, or use personal data in the first place. That stays with the [[GDPR]]: lawfulness of processing, consent, data subject rights. The two regimes overlap on one event: an incident that compromises personal data. When that happens you have two parallel reporting clocks - Article 23 NIS2 to your CSIRT (24h / 72h / one month) and Article 33 GDPR to your [[data protection authority]] (72h). They run side by side, not in sequence. Lesson 3.9 walks through this scenario in full.

## Key takeaways

1. NIS2 is Directive (EU) 2022/2555 - one EU-wide cybersecurity law covering roughly one hundred and sixty thousand entities, replacing NIS1 which was weakly enforced.
2. Three layers of law apply: the NIS2 Directive (goals), the CIR 2024/2690 (detailed technical rules, directly applicable), and your national law (BSIG in Germany) which the national regulator (BSI) enforces.
3. The directive creates personal accountability for the [[management body]] - which is why the leadership team, not just IT, is addressed directly.
4. NIS2 governs data security, not data protection - GDPR keeps that lane. When personal data leaks during a cyber incident, both clocks run in parallel.
