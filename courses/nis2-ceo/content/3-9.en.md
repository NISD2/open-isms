# Lesson 3.9: Scenario - Supplier Breach Affecting Your Customer Data

Sunday night. Your CRM provider emails: security incident, your customer data may be affected, more to follow. The clock started when they pushed send. When your supplier is breached and your customers' data is exposed, the law treats it as your incident.

## The law on supply chain incidents

Article 21(2)(d) NIS2 Directive requires supply chain security as one of your ten mandatory measures - your duty to manage risk through [[vendor|vendors]], not just your own walls. Article 36 adds the customer-facing duty: where an incident may adversely impact your service, you must notify recipients "without undue delay." You can outsource operations. You cannot outsource accountability.

## Walking through the MOVEit case

In May 2023, the Cl0p ransomware group exploited a zero-day in MOVEit, a widely used file-transfer tool. Within weeks, data from over 2,600 organisations was stolen - employee records, customer data, HR files across banks, insurers, government agencies, and consultancies. Breach at the supplier. Data belonged to the customers. Some learned about it from journalists before their own teams confirmed [[exposure]].

**Decision one: force the facts.** The first supplier email is always vague. Do not wait for the next update. Ask immediately: "Which systems of ours were connected, what data was in transit, over what time period?" If they cannot answer, assume the worst and prepare [[customer notification]].

**Decision two: pull the contract.** Specifically: incident notification timelines, [[audit rights]], [[indemnification]]. Those three clauses decide your response timeline, your access rights, and cost recovery. If missing, that is a finding for your next supplier review.

**Decision three: file your own report - on both clocks if personal data is involved.** The supplier's report to their regulator does not cover you. If your customers' data was affected, you file your own Article 23 report - early warning inside twenty-four hours, notification inside seventy-two. And if personal data leaked, Article 33 GDPR fires a separate seventy-two-hour clock to your [[data protection authority]] - that one is not covered by the NIS2 filing. Two parallel timelines, two parallel addressees. Lesson 1.1 explains why these regimes stay separate.

**Decision four: notify customers before the press does.** Under Article 36, tell affected customers yourself within hours of confirming exposure. The template should already exist in your incident response plan. The MOVEit companies that came out cleanest had two things: a pre-drafted notification template and a map of which suppliers touched which customer data. Those cut response time from days to hours.

**Decision five: pursue [[contractual remedy|contractual remedies]] calmly.** The only decision you should take your time on. Once the immediate incident is handled, decide whether to trigger indemnification, switch providers, or renegotiate. Not in the first forty-eight hours.

What went wrong at several MOVEit-affected companies: they waited for the supplier's definitive list of affected records before notifying customers. That list took weeks. By then, customers had read about the breach in the press. MOVEit [[litigation]] ran for years, with settlements from low millions to hundreds of millions of euros.

## What to do before it happens

Map your suppliers to the customer data they touch. That mapping determines whether your response takes hours or weeks.

Review your top five supplier contracts for three clauses: incident notification timelines, audit rights, and indemnification. If any are missing, fix them at the next renewal.

## Key takeaways

1. A [[breach]] at your supplier is your incident under Article 23 whenever your customers are affected - the supplier's report to their regulator does not cover you.
2. Force the facts from the supplier in the first hour, pull the contract in the second, start drafting customer notification in the third.
3. Speed of notification decides the reputational outcome - customers judge you on how fast you told them, not on whose system was breached.
