# Lesson 1.12: The Customer Notification Duty

Your company has just reported a significant incident to the regulator. Two days later your customers read about it in the press. Most leadership teams treat the Article 23 reporting cascade as the full extent of their notification duty. There are two separate customer-facing duties - one that triggers automatically and one the regulator can order - and most companies only know about the second.

## Article 23(2): the self-executing duty

Article 23(2) of the NIS2 Directive requires entities to notify the [[recipients of services]] "[[without undue delay]]" when a significant incident is likely to adversely affect the provision of those services. This duty is [[self-executing notification|self-executing]] - it triggers the moment the entity becomes aware of the [[ongoing threat]]. No regulator order is needed.

If your customers face an active risk - their data may be exposed, they need to change passwords or revoke keys - they need to be told so they can protect themselves. Article 23(2) also requires you to disclose "any measures or [[remedies]] that those recipients are able to take in response to that threat." You do not just tell them there is a problem. You tell them what they can do about it.

## Article 36: regulator-ordered notification

Article 36 gives the competent authority the power to instruct a company to inform its customers about a significant incident. The trigger is the regulator's judgment that [[public interest|public disclosure]] serves the public interest or is necessary to manage the incident.

Article 36 is the floor, not the ceiling. You can always notify proactively before the regulator orders it. Customers who learn about an incident from you will be angry. Customers who learn from the press will not trust you again.

## Two triggers, one principle

The Article 23 cascade (Lesson 1.11) tells the regulator. Article 23(2) and Article 36 tell your customers. They operate independently. The key distinction: Article 23(2) is self-executing and immediate - you must act on your own when there is an ongoing threat. Article 36 is [[regulator-ordered notification|regulator-ordered]]. A company that waits for a regulator order when its customers face an active threat has already failed Article 23(2).

## What to settle before an incident happens

Ask your communications team: "Do we have a pre-drafted customer notification template, and is there a defined [[approval chain]] that ends with the management body?" If the answer is no, build it now. You personally sign off on the notification before it goes out, because under both Article 23(2) and Article 36, the customer notification can become the object of regulator review.

## Key takeaways

1. Article 23(2) is self-executing: when a significant incident is likely to adversely affect your customers, you must notify them without undue delay and tell them what protective measures they can take - no regulator order needed.
2. Article 36 is a separate power: the regulator can order customer notification in the public interest - but waiting for that order when customers face an active threat already breaches Article 23(2).
3. Have a pre-drafted customer notification template and a defined approval chain before an incident happens - the notification itself can become the object of regulator review.
