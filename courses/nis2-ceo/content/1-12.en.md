# Lesson 1.12: The Customer Notification Duty

Your company has just reported a significant incident to the regulator. Two days later your customers read about it in the press. Most leadership teams treat the Article 23 reporting cascade as the full extent of their notification duty. The Directive also points at your customers, in two ways: what you tell them yourself, and what the authority can order you to tell them.

## Article 23(1) and (2): what you tell them yourself

Article 23(1) of the NIS2 Directive says that, where appropriate, entities notify the [[recipients of services]] "[[without undue delay]]" of significant incidents that are likely to adversely affect the provision of those services. Article 23(2) adds a duty for significant cyber threats: tell the recipients who are potentially affected "any measures or [[remedies]] that those recipients are able to take in response to that threat", and where appropriate the threat itself. You do not just tell them there is a problem. You tell them what they can do about it.

How this applies to you depends on your country's law. In Germany, § 35(1) BSIG turns the incident notice into something the BSI can order, and § 35(2) BSIG puts the threat duty on certain sectors only (finance, social security and basic income support, digital infrastructure, ICT service management, digital providers), where the recipients' interests outweigh the entity's.

## What the authority can order

The authority can also make you inform others. Under Article 23(7), where public awareness is needed to prevent or deal with a significant incident, or disclosure is otherwise in the [[public interest]], the CSIRT or the competent authority can, after consulting you, inform the public itself or require you to do so. Under Article 32(4)(e), and Article 33(4)(e) for Important entities, it can order you to inform those potentially affected by a significant cyber threat. In Germany, § 35(1) BSIG lets the BSI order you to inform the recipients of your services about a significant incident.

An order is the floor, not the ceiling. You can always notify proactively before one arrives. Customers who learn about an incident from you will be angry. Customers who learn from the press will not trust you again.

## Two triggers, one principle

The Article 23 cascade (Lesson 1.11) tells the regulator. Article 23(1) and (2) and the authority's orders tell your customers. They operate independently. The key distinction: what Article 23(1) and (2) ask, you act on yourself, as your national law transposes it. An order under Article 23(7) or Article 32(4)(e) is [[regulator-ordered notification|regulator-ordered]]. When your customers need to protect themselves now, waiting for an order is not a plan.

## What to settle before an incident happens

Ask your communications team: "Do we have a pre-drafted customer notification template, and is there a defined [[approval chain]] that ends with the management body?" If the answer is no, build it now. You personally sign off on the notification before it goes out, because whether it follows your own duty or an order, the customer notification can become the object of regulator review.

## Key takeaways

1. Article 23(1) asks you, where appropriate, to tell the recipients of your services without undue delay about a significant incident likely to affect their service, and Article 23(2) to tell those potentially affected by a significant cyber threat what they can do. In Germany, § 35 BSIG makes the first an order of the BSI and limits the second to certain sectors.
2. The authority can require you to inform the public about a significant incident (Article 23(7)) or order you to inform those affected by a significant cyber threat (Article 32(4)(e)) - and you can always notify before it does.
3. Have a pre-drafted customer notification template and a defined approval chain before an incident happens - the notification itself can become the object of regulator review.
