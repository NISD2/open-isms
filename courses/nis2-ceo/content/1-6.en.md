# Lesson 1.6: Your Training Duty

When did you personally last sit through cybersecurity training - not your staff, you? If the answer is "never," you are already in [[breach]] of one of the few NIS2 duties that is addressed to you by name. Article 20(2) is the clause that places the training obligation on every member of the leadership team individually. It is the reason this course exists.

## What the rule means

Article 20(2) of the NIS2 Directive states: "Member States shall ensure that the members of the management bodies of essential and important entities are required to follow training ... in order that they gain sufficient knowledge and skills to enable them to identify risks and assess cybersecurity risk-management practices and their impact on the services provided by the entity."

Three [[competence areas|competence areas]] in that sentence. First: identifying risks - you need to understand what threats your company faces. Second: assessing the measures used to manage those risks - you need to evaluate whether what your team has put in place actually works. Third: understanding how those risks and measures affect the services your company provides - you need to connect cybersecurity to business operations. Covering only one or two of these fails the standard.

## Frequency and documentation

The directive says training must be "regular." It does not define the word. In Germany, the floor is every three years; the BSI recommends annual in practice. The directive does not specify a duration - the BSI estimates roughly four hours of [[equivalent content]] as sufficient. Training can be delivered by an external provider, by a consultancy, or by qualified internal [[personnel]]. There is no exam required by law. The regulator judges sufficiency based on three things: what was covered, for how long, and how it was documented.

The [[training duty]] is personal and [[non-delegable]]. You cannot send your CISO, your lawyer, or your assistant to attend on your behalf. The one thing you must keep is the [[documentation]]: who attended, for how long, what was covered. That record is what the regulator asks for in an audit.

## When a fresh round is required

"Regular" sets the floor, not the ceiling. Four practical events should prompt a fresh training round between the scheduled re-takes. First, a change in the composition of the management body - a new board member or managing director who has not yet received the Article 20(2) coverage. Second, a significant change in business processes - a new product line, a new geography, an acquisition, a major outsourcing shift. Third, a significant change in your risk exposure - a new sector, a clear shift in the threat landscape, a reportable incident at your company or at a comparable peer. Fourth, a significant change in the implemented or planned risk-management measures - a new ISMS, a major supplier swap, an architecture change. Each of these means the training that was sufficient yesterday no longer maps to the risks and measures the leadership team now has to assess.

## What to ask your team

"Do we have a record of the last time every member of the management body completed cybersecurity training, and does it document the content covered and the time spent?" If the answer is no, you have no evidence that you fulfilled the duty, even if you did.

## Key takeaways

1. Training must cover three competence areas: identifying risks, assessing the risk-management measures, and assessing their impact on the services your company provides - covering only one or two fails the standard.
2. "Regular" means at minimum every three years in Germany, with annual recommended by the BSI - and the training duty is personal and non-delegable.
3. The auditor judges sufficiency based on content covered, time spent, and documentation retained - not on which body issued a certificate.
4. Four practical events should prompt a fresh round between scheduled re-takes: a change in the management body, a significant change in business processes, a significant change in risk exposure, or a significant change in the implemented or planned risk-management measures.
