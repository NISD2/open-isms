# Lesson 1.6: Your Training Duty

When did you personally last sit through cybersecurity training - not your staff, you? If the answer is "never," you are already in [[breach]] of one of the few NIS2 duties that is addressed to you by name. Article 20(2) is the clause that places the training obligation on every member of the leadership team individually. It is the reason this course exists.

## What the rule means

Article 20(2) of the NIS2 Directive states: "Member States shall ensure that the members of the management bodies of essential and important entities are required to follow training ... in order that they gain sufficient knowledge and skills to enable them to identify risks and assess cybersecurity risk-management practices and their impact on the services provided by the entity."

Three [[competence areas|competence areas]] in that sentence. First: identifying risks - you need to understand what threats your company faces. Second: assessing the measures used to manage those risks - you need to evaluate whether what your team has put in place actually works. Third: understanding how those risks and measures affect the services your company provides - you need to connect cybersecurity to business operations. Covering only one or two of these fails the standard.

## Frequency and documentation

The directive requires the members of the management body to follow training and names no interval; in Article 20(2), "on a regular basis" refers to the training companies are encouraged to offer their employees. In Germany, Section 38(3) BSIG requires management to attend training "regularly", also without an interval, so there is no legal floor. The BSI recommends a thorough initial training followed by regular follow-up sessions. The directive does not specify a duration, and the BSI sets no minimum either: duration and interval follow the company's risk exposure and the individual skills of its management. Its curriculum for management training of 28 October 2026, expressly non-binding, works through an example of four hours without exercises across six modules, or about three hours if participants read a short preparation paper beforehand and that is documented. Training can be delivered by an external provider, by a consultancy, or by qualified internal [[personnel]]. There is no exam required by law. The regulator judges sufficiency based on three things: what was covered, for how long, and how it was documented.

The [[training duty]] is personal and [[non-delegable]]. You cannot send your CISO, your lawyer, or your assistant to attend on your behalf. The training is mandatory for every managing director. The BSI recommends also including people who take part in or prepare risk-management decisions, such as the CISO or information security officer, finance and operations leads, legal and compliance, OT leads and authorised signatories (Prokuristen). Their training does not replace yours.

What you must keep is the [[documentation]]. The BSI curriculum lists the minimum content of the training record: date, time and duration, the provider or internal unit, the participants with their role or function, the content covered, and the reference to Section 38(3) BSIG. Add a participant list of all managing directors, signed or confirmed digitally, and the training material with version and date. That record is what the regulator asks for in an audit.

## When a fresh round is required

"Regular" sets the floor, not the ceiling. Four practical events should prompt a fresh training round between the scheduled re-takes. First, a change in the composition of the management body - a new board member or managing director who has not yet received the Article 20(2) coverage. Second, a significant change in business processes - a new product line, a new geography, an acquisition, a major outsourcing shift. Third, a significant change in your risk exposure - a new sector, a clear shift in the threat landscape, a reportable incident at your company or at a comparable peer. Fourth, a significant change in the implemented or planned risk-management measures - a new ISMS, a major supplier swap, an architecture change. Each of these means the training that was sufficient yesterday no longer maps to the risks and measures the leadership team now has to assess.

## What to ask your team

"Do we have a record of the last time every member of the management body completed cybersecurity training, and does it document the content covered, the time spent, the provider and each participant's role?" If the answer is no, you have no evidence that you fulfilled the duty, even if you did.

## Key takeaways

1. Training must cover three competence areas: identifying risks, assessing the risk-management measures, and assessing their impact on the services your company provides - covering only one or two fails the standard.
2. Neither the directive nor Section 38(3) BSIG ("regularly") sets a fixed interval or a minimum duration; the BSI recommends setting duration and interval by your risk exposure, and its non-binding curriculum works through an example of four hours. The training duty is personal and non-delegable.
3. The auditor judges sufficiency based on content covered, time spent, and documentation retained - not on which body issued a certificate. The BSI lists for the record: date, time, duration, provider, participants with role, content, and the reference to Section 38(3) BSIG.
4. Four practical events should prompt a fresh round between scheduled re-takes: a change in the management body, a significant change in business processes, a significant change in risk exposure, or a significant change in the implemented or planned risk-management measures.
