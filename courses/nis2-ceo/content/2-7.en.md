# Lesson 2.7: Measure 2 - Incident Handling

It is four o'clock on a Friday afternoon. Your IT manager calls and says "I think we have a problem." Three questions decide the next seventy-two hours: who do you call, what do you shut down, and are you allowed to pay. If you do not know the answer to all three before the call happens, your [[incident response plan]] has already failed.

## What this measure means

Article 21(2)(b) of the NIS2 Directive lists "incident handling" as one of your ten measures. The written document at the centre of this measure is called the incident response plan. It has five parts, and the auditor checks whether all five exist and have been tested: detection (how you notice something is wrong), classification (how serious it is and whether it crosses the reporting threshold from Lesson 1.11), response (who does what, in what order), recovery (how you restore service - this ties into Measure 3, business continuity), and [[lessons learned]] (what happened, why, and what changes as a result).

The classification section matters most to you as CEO, because it is the section that decides whether the twenty-four-hour reporting clock under Article 23 starts ticking. If the classification rules are vague or missing, your team will waste the first hours debating whether to report instead of actually responding.

## What happens if you don't have it

The CEO-specific part of the plan is the list of decisions only you can make: declaring a crisis, authorising emergency spending, approving external communication to customers and the regulator, and deciding whether to pay a ransom. If those decisions are not pre-assigned in the plan by name, the first hours of a real incident are wasted working out who has authority.

The plan must also be tested. A paper rehearsal against a scenario is called a [[tabletop exercise]]. If your last tabletop was more than twelve months ago, or if nothing was changed as a result, the auditor treats the exercise as decorative. BSI IT-Grundschutz DER.2.1.A17 requires the lessons learned from incidents to be reported to management annually - if that annual report does not exist, the auditor notes it.

## What to ask your team

Two questions. "In our incident response plan, which decisions are reserved for me by name?" If your name does not appear in the plan, the management layer is missing. "When did we last run a tabletop exercise, and what did we change afterwards?" If the answer is vague, book thirty minutes with your IT manager and your lawyer - walk through one ransomware scenario, identify every decision point, write the decisions down, and sign the update.

## Key takeaways

1. An incident response plan has five parts - detection, classification, response, recovery, and lessons learned - and the classification section must align with the Article 23 reporting deadlines.
2. The CEO's decisions - declaring a crisis, authorising spending, approving communications, the ransom question - must be pre-assigned in the plan by name.
3. DER.2.1.A17 requires an annual lessons-learned report to management; if it does not exist, the auditor treats the learning loop as missing.
