# Lesson 4.4: Common Policy Exclusions to Read For

In 2017, Mondelez filed a one-hundred-million-dollar [[cyber insurance]] claim after the NotPetya attack. Zurich refused to pay, citing the [[acts-of-war]] exclusion. The case ran for nearly five years and settled for an undisclosed amount. A single clause decided the outcome of a nine-figure claim - and your policies contain the same clauses.

## Why exclusions matter more than coverage promises

An insurance policy is a promise to pay under specific conditions. The exclusions are the conditions that turn that promise off. Every [[exclusion clause|exclusion]] is a claim your insurer will not pay, regardless of the [[premium]] you paid or the coverage your [[broker]] described.

## D&O exclusions to read for

Read your D&O policy for these specific clauses.

**The cyber liability exclusion**: sometimes explicit, sometimes buried in broader language. If present, your D&O does not cover NIS2 claims against you personally - the single biggest gap.

**The pre-existing condition exclusion**: blocks claims from anything the insured knew about before the policy started.

**The [[sanction|sanctions]] exclusion**: blocks any claim touching sanctioned countries or entities.

**The acts-of-war exclusion**: became significantly broader after the Mondelez ruling. Lloyd's of London formally tightened this language in 2023.

**The failure-to-maintain-security clause**: can [[void]] coverage if you did not keep up standard controls.

**The late [[notification clause|notification]] clause**: voids coverage if you file the claim with the insurer after the policy's strict deadline.

## Cyber insurance exclusions to read for

Read your cyber insurance policy for these specific clauses.

**Pre-existing vulnerabilities and unpatched systems**: excludes anything that was known and not fixed before the incident - a common reason claims are denied.

**The acts-of-war or [[nation-state]] attack exclusion**: Lloyd's tightened this wording in 2023 following the Mondelez case. Every EU cyber policy written since then contains narrower language around state-attributed attacks.

**Sanctions law compliance**: affects whether the insurer can legally pay a [[ransomware|ransom]].

**Insured-side failures**: excludes incidents caused by your own staff in certain ways.

**Regulator fines and penalties**: almost universally excluded from cyber policies. Your cyber insurance does not pay NIS2 fines.

## What to ask your broker

Ask for the exact wording of each clause above before you sign the next renewal. For the acts-of-war clause specifically, ask three questions: how is "hostile or warlike action" defined in this policy, what happens when [[attribution]] is unclear, and what is the insurer's stated position on state-aligned ransomware groups. Get the answers in writing. The first time most CEOs read these clauses is the day the [[denial]] letter arrives - not before.

## Key takeaways

1. A single exclusion clause can decide a nine-figure claim - the Mondelez case is the clearest public proof.
2. Read the specific clauses by name: cyber exclusion, acts-of-war, sanctions, pre-existing conditions, failure to maintain security, and late notification.
3. Ask your broker in writing how each clause applies to specific scenarios - not whether you are "generally" covered.
