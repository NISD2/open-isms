# Lesson 3.6: CISO and Supervisory Board Reporting Lines

Most CEOs assume the CISO should sit under IT because cybersecurity is a technical discipline. The EU rulebook says the opposite. At least one person must report directly to the management body on cybersecurity matters, and most CISOs in mid-sized companies today do not meet that standard. The question of where the CISO sits on the [[organisational chart]] sounds like corporate plumbing, but it is binding in the EU implementing regulation for the digital providers it covers, and for every other covered company it is the clearest benchmark of good oversight. A supervisor can look at the line on the organisational chart.

## What the requirement means

Commission Implementing Regulation 2024/2690, Annex point 1.2.3, states: "At least one person shall report directly to the management bodies on matters of network and information system security." Somewhere in your company there has to be one named person whose reporting line goes straight to the management body, without an IT director or finance director in between, specifically for cybersecurity matters. For the digital providers named in Article 1 of the regulation (lesson 1.1) this is binding. For every other covered company it is not a legal requirement in this form, but it is the line to aim for.

The reason is practical, not bureaucratic. A CISO reporting to IT has no authority over the rest of the company - including HR, finance, sales, and operations. A CISO reporting directly to you inherits your authority. When they say "HR needs to change the onboarding process to include security training," HR knows that instruction is backed by the management body. The same instruction sent through IT is a suggestion from a middle manager. The structural difference determines whether security decisions are executed across the company or negotiated department by department.

## What happens if you don't have it

If the CISO reports through IT or finance, the requirement (for digital providers) or the benchmark (for everyone else) is not met - regardless of how competent the CISO is or how often they informally brief you. The auditor can trace the line on the organisational chart. If there are layers between the CISO and the management body, that can be recorded as a [[finding]]. If the company then has an incident, the absence of a [[direct reporting line]] is evidence that the management body was not properly overseeing the cybersecurity programme.

For companies with a [[supervisory board]], the same principle applies at that level: the supervisory board should receive summary cybersecurity reports at least annually, covering status, major incidents, risk landscape, budget, and regulatory changes.

## What to ask your team

Pull the organisational chart and count the layers between the CISO and the management body. If the number is more than zero, the structure does not meet the requirement or benchmark - propose a direct reporting line at the next board meeting. The budget and headcount do not have to change, only the line on the chart. Then schedule a recurring monthly meeting with the CISO and agree on a standing written agenda. That one-page written summary, produced every meeting, is what you show the auditor.

## Key takeaways

1. Commission Implementing Regulation 2024/2690, Annex 1.2.3 requires at least one person to report directly to the management body on cybersecurity matters - binding for the digital providers in its Article 1, the benchmark for everyone else.
2. A CISO reporting through IT or finance does not meet it, no matter how competent the CISO is.
3. The reporting meetings must be regular, documented, and contain specific content - [[key performance indicator|KPIs]], [[key risk indicator|KRIs]], incident summary, budget status, threat landscape, and decisions needing [[escalation]].
