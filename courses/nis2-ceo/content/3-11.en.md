# Lesson 3.11: Scenario - Pre-Audit Gap Discovery

The worst moment in a regulator audit is not when the auditor finds something. It is when the auditor finds something you already knew about and hid. Two weeks before your audit, your team finds a security [[gap]] open for six months. Hide it, quietly fix it, or disclose it - the choice is one of the sharpest judgment tests the law puts in front of you.

## The legal foundation for finding your own gaps

Article 32(4) NIS2 gives regulators the power to inspect on-site, request information, and interview staff - they cross-check what you told them against what they find. Article 21(2)(f) requires "policies and procedures to assess the effectiveness of cybersecurity risk-management measures" - the law expects you to find your own gaps. Regulators distinguish between gaps found and fixed versus gaps hidden. Found and fixed proves your process works. Hidden is bad faith - and bad faith turns a remediable finding into a [[sanction]].

## Walking through the three options

Two weeks before your regulator audit. Your CISO says: "Backups of the customer database have been stored without encryption for six months. Nobody spotted it."

You have three options.

**Option A: Hide it.** Tell nobody, hope the auditor looks elsewhere. Logs survive longer than people think. When an auditor discovers [[concealment]] - a log entry, an old backup, an employee interview - the cover-up becomes a separate violation. Every future audit gets harder because the regulator now assumes you hide things. Rule this out explicitly.

**Option B: Fix it, say nothing.** Encrypt the backups, update the procedure, walk in with the problem solved. But timestamps exist, old unencrypted archives survive, and someone may mention the fix in an interview. The auditor pieces the timeline together: "we had a gap, fixed it, and did not mention it" sounds like concealment even if unintended.

**Option C: Fix it and disclose it.** Encrypt immediately. Document the discovery, cause, timeline, and fix. Then you personally write to the auditor before the audit - what was found, when, what was done, what the new controls look like. The letter comes from you, the CEO. Authorise the budget in the same meeting you learn about the gap - not next week.

EU regulators have stated in writing that they prefer found-and-fixed gaps to hidden ones. [[Good-faith disclosure]] typically results in a [[remediation order]] or warning, not a fine. Discovered concealment typically results in a fine plus ongoing monitoring, and the CEO's credibility with the regulator is damaged for years.

## What this means for your position

The difference between Option A and Option C is usually the difference between a two-line entry in the audit report and a line item in your annual accounts. An auditor who trusts you gives you the benefit of the doubt on borderline findings. An auditor who has caught you hiding something treats every borderline finding as a potential [[cover-up]].

## Key takeaways

1. Finding your own gaps is the expected state - it is what the law means by "policies to assess effectiveness." The regulator is not surprised when a gap exists. They care about what you did after you found it.
2. Hiding a gap or quietly fixing it both fail when the [[audit trail]] tells the story - and the audit trail always tells the story.
3. Good-faith [[disclosure]], signed by the CEO, with the fix already in place, is the only option that strengthens your position in front of the regulator.
