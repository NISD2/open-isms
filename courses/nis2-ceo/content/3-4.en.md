# Lesson 3.4: Red Flags in Your Team's Sign-Off Process

Nine sentences. If your team says any of them when they put a security document in front of you, the correct response is to put the pen down and ask a harder question. The previous lessons covered what you are signing and when to re-sign. This lesson covers the signals that the process behind the signature has already failed - and what to say when you hear them.

## Why refusing to sign is your strongest tool

Article 20(1) of the NIS2 Directive requires you personally to approve the cybersecurity risk-management measures. That approval has to be informed - as Lesson 3.2 covered, a signature you cannot defend as informed is legally worthless and in court it works against you. Refusing to sign is not a bureaucratic delay. It is the tool the law gave you to force the right work to happen before your name goes on a document.

The rule for this lesson is simple: if the explanation feels faster than the document, the process is broken. Refusing costs hours. Signing blind costs years. When your team learns you will ask these questions every time, the quality of what they bring to the table improves permanently.

## Nine things your team should never say

Picture the quarterly security meeting. Watch for these sentences.

"We have always done it this way." There is no documented review process and no one knows who changed what. You reply: "Show me the [[version history]]."

"Sign here, it is just a [[formality]]." Translation: they do not take the signature seriously, which means you should not either. You reply: "If it is a formality, why does it need my signature at all?"

"Our IT department handles this." Nobody from leadership is involved, making your [[sign-off]] uninformed by definition. You reply: "I am leadership. That makes me the person who has to understand it, not IT."

"We cannot afford that control." They are accepting a risk without analysis. You reply: "Show me the risk assessment that says this is an acceptable [[residual risk|residual]] risk and who approved it."

"We have never had a problem." They are using the absence of past incidents as evidence of present control - that is [[survivor bias]]. You reply: "That is not an answer. Show me the current test results."

"It is all documented in the system, but you cannot access it." You are being asked to sign something you cannot read. You reply: "Then I will wait until I can read it."

"Just trust the [[auditor]]." What they are really saying: they are confusing the auditor's role with yours. The auditor is checking you, not the other way around. You reply: "The auditor has a job and I have a job. This is mine."

"We will fix it after the audit." The gap is known and the plan is to hide it. You reply: "If it is known, it is already a [[finding]]. Fix it now and I will sign the fix."

"The previous CEO signed something similar." This means they are trying to borrow someone else's judgment. You reply: "The previous CEO is not signing this document. I am."

## Key takeaways

1. Refusing to sign an uninformed document is your most powerful lever for forcing your team to work properly - use it.
2. Each red-flag sentence has a concrete counter-question that moves the burden back onto the person asking you to sign.
3. A delayed sign-off costs your team hours. A [[void]] sign-off costs you years. The trade is not close.
