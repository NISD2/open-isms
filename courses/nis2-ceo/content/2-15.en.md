# Lesson 2.15: Measure 10 - Multi-Factor Authentication and Secured Communications

The single biggest reason account takeovers succeed is that [[multi-factor authentication (MFA)|multi-factor authentication]] was not turned on. Article 21(2)(j) of the NIS2 Directive names it as a required control - and the first person who must be using it is you.

## What this measure means

Article 21(2)(j) requires "the use of multi-factor authentication or continuous authentication solutions, secured voice, video and text communications and secured emergency communication systems within the entity, where appropriate." Multi-factor authentication - MFA - means logging in requires two things: something you know (a password) and something you have (a phone, a hardware token). A stolen password alone is no longer enough.

Four categories where MFA is effectively mandatory for a NIS2 entity: remote access, administrator accounts, critical systems (databases, backups, payment systems), and email. The "where appropriate" qualifier in the directive gives flexibility at the margins, but for those four categories in an in-scope entity, the flexibility is gone.

The second half of the clause covers [[secured communications]]. Voice, video, and text channels used for sensitive business must be encrypted. And a secured emergency communication channel must exist for when the normal systems are compromised - if ransomware has taken down your email, how does the crisis team talk to each other? An [[out-of-band channel]] (a separate messaging tool, a dedicated phone line) exists for exactly that moment. If the answer to "what is our emergency channel" is "email," the answer is wrong.

## What happens if you don't have it

BSI Standard 200-1 Principle 6 names the management body's lead-by-example duty: you must personally follow the security controls you approved. For MFA, this means your own account is the lead-by-example test - if it is exempted, the audit will see it. A CEO who approved MFA for the company but exempted their own login has created a personal audit finding. Lesson 2.12 covers the broader lead-by-example principle for training and phishing simulations - here the point is specific: your account, your MFA, today.

## What to ask your team

Check your own email account today - is MFA turned on? If not, turn it on. Ten minutes.

Then ask your IT manager: "Is MFA currently required on all administrator accounts, all remote access paths, and the backup system?" The answer is yes or no. If it is anything other than yes, set a one-month deadline. Then: "What is our out-of-band emergency communication channel, and when did we last test it?"

## Key takeaways

1. MFA is effectively mandatory on administrator accounts, remote access, critical systems, and email - the "where appropriate" qualifier has no practical flexibility for those four categories.
2. Secured emergency communications require an out-of-band channel that works when normal systems are down - if the crisis plan relies on email, it fails when it matters.
3. BSI Standard 200-1 Principle 6 makes your own account the lead-by-example test - a CEO who exempts themselves from MFA has created a personal finding.
