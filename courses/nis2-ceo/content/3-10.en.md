# Lesson 3.10: Scenario - Phishing Success: Significant or Not?

A junior employee just typed her [[credential|credentials]] into a fake login page. Phishing is the entry point for sixty percent of successful cyber attacks in Europe, but only a fraction cross the legal reporting [[threshold]]. Four questions separate routine [[containment]] from a filing to your regulator within twenty-four hours.

## The legal threshold for reporting

Article 23(3) NIS2 defines a [[significant incident]] as one causing or capable of causing severe operational disruption or financial loss, or affecting other persons by causing considerable damage. Either arm triggers reporting.

CIR 2024/2690, Article 3 sets the numbers. Significant if any one is true: financial loss exceeds five hundred thousand euros or five percent of [[turnover]], whichever is lower; trade secrets exfiltrated; death; considerable health damage; or suspectedly malicious unauthorised access causing severe disruption.

Article 4 adds the [[recurring incidents rule]]: two or more incidents in six months sharing the same root cause that together cross the threshold count as one significant incident. Small incidents become reportable retroactively. "Phishing" is not the test. The consequences are.

## Walking through the scenario

Tuesday, 9:30 AM. Your CISO says a junior accounts-payable clerk entered credentials into a fake Microsoft login page last night. IT detected it at 8 AM - suspicious sign-in from a foreign country. Account locked. Do you file?

**First question: has the attacker used the credentials?** Often "not yet" - credentials are harvested and sold first, used weeks later. This tells you: containment or active response.

**Second question: what did those credentials access?** Email only - contained, probably not reportable. Log it, force a company-wide password reset, move on. Finance systems - reporting becomes a real possibility. Customer databases - almost certainly reportable, plus a notification duty under Article 36.

**Third question: was data [[exfiltration|exfiltrated]]?** Confirmation takes hours to days. The early warning within twenty-four hours allows you to state what is still uncertain. You need a best current assessment, not a final answer.

**Fourth question: does this cross the CIR thresholds?** Total expected loss - [[forensics]], downtime, [[remediation]], notification - against five hundred thousand euros or five percent of turnover, whichever is lower. Check for trade secret exfiltration and malicious access to operational systems. Any one crosses the line, you file.

Clean version: [[permission map|permissions]] already mapped - email and invoicing portal only. Forensics confirmed no exfiltration within six hours. Logged, contained, not reported, with a written [[decision tree|decision log]].

Messy version: no permission map. The company could not determine whether the attacker reached customer data. That uncertainty required a [[protective filing]] - not because the incident was severe, but because they could not prove it was not.

## What to do under uncertainty

If you are genuinely uncertain whether the thresholds are met, file the early warning. The regulator prefers "we are not sure, here is what we know" over silence. You can update the report as facts emerge. You cannot retroactively file one that should have been sent two days ago.

## Key takeaways

1. Four questions decide whether a phishing success is reportable: did the attacker use the credentials, what did they reach, was data exfiltrated, does the financial impact cross the threshold.
2. The CIR thresholds are precise - five hundred thousand euros or five percent of turnover (whichever is lower), trade secret exfiltration in any amount, death, considerable health damage, or malicious unauthorised access causing severe disruption.
3. When genuinely uncertain, file the early warning - the regulator prefers a preliminary report that updates later over a retroactive one that should have been filed sooner.
