# Lesson 4.1: Insurance Is Not a Substitute for Controls

In 2017, the food company Mondelez was hit by the NotPetya [[malware]] and suffered roughly one hundred million dollars in damage. Mondelez held a [[cyber insurance|cyber policy]] with Zurich Insurance for that amount. Zurich refused the [[claim]], arguing NotPetya was an act of war. Five years of litigation in Illinois state court followed. The [[settlement]] was confidential. If you think your insurance policy is your NIS2 plan, this lesson explains why it is not.

## What the rule means

Article 21 of the NIS2 Directive lists ten measures you must have in place. Nowhere in those ten measures does the word "insurance" appear. Article 32 sets fines up to ten million euros or two percent of global turnover. Nowhere in the fine structure is insurance treated as a mitigation. When the auditor asks "did you do your job," the evidence they check is [[controls]], processes, sign-offs, [[training records]], and [[management reviews]]. An insurance certificate is not on that list.

As Lesson 2.3 covered, the BSI guidance explicitly says risk transfer - including insurance - is not an acceptable treatment for serious risks at NIS2-covered entities. Insurance pays after the incident. It does not prevent it. The attack still happens. The downtime still happens. The data still leaks. Insurance spreads the financial loss after the fact - and only the financial loss, subject to [[exclusion|exclusions]] the insurer's lawyers wrote.

## Why you care

Three things insurance does not remove. [[Regulatory fines]] under Article 32 - most policies explicitly exclude them. The manager ban under Article 34 - no [[policy]] covers that. The [[customer notification duty]] under Article 36 - that lands on you regardless. And [[personal liability]] under Article 20(1) is covered by [[D&O insurance]], not cyber insurance - they are different products. Module 4 (Lessons 4.1 through 4.3) covers the insurance landscape in detail: what D&O covers, how it differs from cyber insurance, and which exclusions to check before renewal.

The Mondelez case proves the pattern: even a hundred-million-dollar policy can be denied under a single [[exclusion clause]] when the insurer decides the attack qualifies as an [[acts of war|act of war]].

## What to ask your team

One question to close Module 2: "If we removed our cyber insurance policy tomorrow, which of the ten NIS2 measures would still be in place?" If the answer is "all of them," you have a programme. If the answer is "we are counting on the policy for some of them," you have a gap.

## Key takeaways

1. Insurance pays after the incident; the [[regulator]] audits what you did before it - nothing in the ten measures of Article 21 is satisfied by an insurance certificate.
2. The Mondelez v Zurich case proved that even a large policy can be denied under a single exclusion clause - the [[coverage]] you think you have and the coverage you actually have are not the same.
3. Regulatory fines, the manager ban, and customer notification are personal liabilities no insurance policy removes - Module 4 covers the insurance detail.
