# Lesson 1.8: The Business Judgment Rule Does Not Protect You

When leadership teams learn about personal liability under NIS2, the first response is always: "The Business Judgment Rule protects us." It does not.

## The rule and its limits

The [[Business Judgment Rule]] protects directors from personal liability for business decisions made in good faith, with reasonable information, for the company's benefit. Every EU country has some version of this rule.

The rule covers choices about strategy, investment, product, pricing. It does not cover statutory duties. When the law tells you to do something, you do it. Corporate law across the EU draws this line sharply. The principle is called the [[duty of legality]]: a director must comply with applicable law, and no business judgment is permitted about whether to follow a legal duty.

Article 20(1) and Article 21 of the NIS2 Directive create [[statutory duty|statutory duties]]. Those duties fall under the duty of legality, not the Business Judgment Rule. "I made a reasonable business decision not to fully implement Article 21" is not a valid [[defence]]. The rule may protect your choice of how to implement - which vendor, which standard, which budget split - but it cannot protect a decision on whether to implement.

## Why US enforcement illustrates the principle

In 2023, the SEC charged the CISO of SolarWinds personally after a supply-chain breach that had sat inside federal networks for nine months. The SEC relied on internal documents - Slack messages, security assessments, [[risk report|risk reports]] - showing a gap between external [[disclosure|disclosures]] and internal reporting. The claim was not that a business judgment failed. The claim was that legally required [[internal controls]] and disclosures were missing.

This is US securities law, not EU NIS2. But the principle is identical: when the legal duty says "have the controls, disclose the posture," deciding not to is not a protected judgment call. By late 2025, SolarWinds had reported more than $100M in direct incident costs, before any personal settlement.

Three decision points from the case translate directly to NIS2. First: internal risk reports named control gaps, but leadership did not close them. Under NIS2, Article 21 requires controls "appropriate to the risk." A known gap the company declines to close is a [[compliance decision|compliance failure]], not a business judgment. Second: the company's external description of its security posture diverged from internal reports. Article 20(1) requires the management body to oversee the measures - the internal picture and the approved picture must match. Third: risk information was not reaching leadership in actionable form - exactly the governance flow Article 20(1) requires.

## The question that protects you

At every leadership meeting where cybersecurity measures are reviewed, ask: "Are there gaps between what our internal risk reports say and what we have implemented?" If the answer is yes, closing those gaps is a legal duty. Document the question and the action taken - the [[due care]] record that protects you if the question is ever asked in [[court]].

## Key takeaways

1. The Business Judgment Rule protects commercial decisions, not compliance with law - NIS2 Article 20 and Article 21 create statutory duties under the duty of legality, not business judgment.
2. "I made a reasonable business decision not to implement the measure" is not a valid defence; the rule protects how you implement, not whether you implement.
3. Internal documents - risk reports, assessments, meeting minutes - are what the regulator uses to prove you knew about the gap. The distance between what you knew and what you did is the evidence.
