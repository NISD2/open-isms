# Lesson 1.3: Penalties and the Article 34 Manager Ban

Ten million euros. Or two percent of your company's global annual revenue, whichever is higher - the maximum company fine under NIS2 for Essential entities, and only the first of three different consequences the regulator can impose. Most leadership teams focus on the company fine and stop there. This lesson explains all three layers of [[exposure]] and why the company fine is the smallest problem a CEO should be worrying about.

## Three layers of exposure

Article 32 of the NIS2 Directive sets the maximum [[administrative fine]] for Essential entities at the higher of ten million euros or two percent of total worldwide annual [[turnover]]. For Important entities, the cap is the higher of seven million euros or one point four percent of worldwide turnover. Member states can set higher fines than these directive minimums.

The company fine is exposure number one. Exposure number two is Article 34, which gives regulators the power to ban an individual from holding any management position. The trigger is repeated or serious violations. The ban is not limited to the company where the violation occurred - it can apply to all management positions. Article 34 also includes [[interim]] management restrictions: a temporary removal from office while the investigation is still running. You can be removed before the case is decided.

Exposure number three is your country's corporate law, which allows the company itself to sue its own directors for damages caused by failure to fulfil [[statutory duties]]. Lesson 1.7 covers this chain in detail. These three layers stack. The regulator can fine the company, ban you personally, and the company can sue you for damages - all for the same failure. Article 20(1) makes this explicit: management bodies "can be held liable for infringements."

## What happens after the fine

Consider this sequence. Your company has been fined six million euros for failing to implement the measures listed in Article 21. First, the company pays the fine. Second, the regulator reviews whether the failure triggers a [[manager ban]] under Article 34 - and an interim restriction can already remove you from office while [[proceedings]] run. Third, the company's shareholders review whether to sue you personally under corporate law for the damage.

If you have no evidence that you tried to understand your duties - no training record, no documented [[sign-off]], no meeting minutes showing you asked the questions - the factor that tips all three layers in the wrong direction.

## What to ask your legal counsel

"What is my maximum personal exposure if the company suffers a NIS2 fine and then sues me under corporate law?" The [[cumulative sanction|cumulative]] cap on personal financial exposure is uncapped in many EU member states. Asking the question and documenting that you asked it is already part of the [[due care]] standard.

## Key takeaways

1. The company fine caps at ten million euros or two percent of global revenue for Essential entities and seven million euros or one point four percent for Important entities - and member states can go higher.
2. Article 34 gives the regulator the power to ban you, personally, from holding any management position - including an interim removal while proceedings are still running.
3. Your national corporate law lets the company sue you personally for damages on top of both the fine and the ban - and in many EU jurisdictions the burden of proof runs against you (Lesson 1.7).
