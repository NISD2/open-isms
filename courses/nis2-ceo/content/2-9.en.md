# Lesson 2.9: Measure 4 - Supply Chain Security

In December 2020, a software company called SolarWinds pushed a routine update to roughly eighteen thousand customers. The update contained a back door planted by attackers. Every one of those customers had to treat it as their own incident, not their supplier's. Your suppliers' security is your security, and you are liable.

## What this measure means

Article 21(2)(d) of the NIS2 Directive requires "supply chain security, including security-related aspects concerning the relationships between each entity and its direct suppliers or service providers." The duty covers anyone with access to your systems or your data - from your cloud provider and IT outsourcer to the cleaning company with a key to the server room. If they can touch your data or your infrastructure, they are a supplier under the directive.

The directive and the CIR expect four things for every supplier. One: a [[supplier inventory]] - who they are, what they do, what they can access. Two: a risk classification - not every supplier is equally critical, and the classification drives how much scrutiny they get. Three: contractual clauses - incident notification so they tell you fast when they are breached, a security baseline, and [[audit rights]] so you or a nominee can check their work. Four: periodic review - audits, questionnaires, or certificates confirming they still meet the baseline.

## What happens if you don't have it

The pattern of the last five years is clear: SolarWinds in 2020, Kaseya in 2021, MOVEit in 2023. The attackers did not break into the customers - they broke into a supplier, and the supplier's own systems did the rest. If your supplier is breached and your customers' data is exposed, the reporting duty under Article 23 and the customer notification duty under Article 36 both land on you, not on the supplier. Lesson 3.9 walks through a full supplier breach scenario.

## What to ask your team

Before you sign any supplier renewal, ask: "Is this supplier on our critical list, and when was their last security review?" If there is no critical-supplier list, that is the first action item. Then check the contract for three clauses: incident notification within a defined window, audit rights, and a security baseline that references a named standard rather than "reasonable security." If any clause is missing, send the draft back before signing.

For the periodic review itself, ENISA publishes [[supply chain|supply chain]] security guidance with structured questions you can send to suppliers to assess their security posture. You do not need to build the questionnaire from scratch. The nisd2.eu supplier portal offers a free, digitised version of this assessment - your supplier fills it in online, and you get the results in a format the auditor can read. Whether you use a spreadsheet, a PDF, or a portal, the point is the same: the assessment has to happen, and the results have to be documented.

## Key takeaways

1. "Supplier" under NIS2 means anyone with access to your systems or data - far broader than procurement, and including physical access providers.
2. Four things for each supplier: inventory entry, risk classification, contractual security clauses (notification, audit rights, baseline), and periodic review.
3. If your supplier is breached and your data leaks, the reporting and notification duties land on you - the supplier's report does not cover you.
