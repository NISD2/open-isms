# Lesson 2.8: Measure 3 - Business Continuity

In June 2017, the shipping company Maersk lost its entire global IT infrastructure to a single malware strain in under two hours. The recovery cost roughly three hundred million euros - and the only reason it took ten days instead of months was one backup that happened to be offline due to a power cut in Ghana. Luck is not a control.

## What this measure means

Article 21(2)(c) of the NIS2 Directive requires "business continuity, such as backup management and disaster recovery, and crisis management." Three distinct components, not one.

Backup management: copies of your data and systems, stored so that if the original is destroyed or encrypted, you have something to restore from. The substance is simple - backups must exist, they must be stored separately from the systems they protect, and they must be tested regularly. An untested backup is not a backup.

Disaster recovery: the procedures and infrastructure that bring the business back online after a major event. Two numbers drive every recovery plan. [[RTO]] - Recovery Time Objective - is how quickly you must be back up. [[RPO]] - Recovery Point Objective - is how much data you can afford to lose. If your RTO for your core system is four hours and your last restore test took three days, the plan does not match reality.

Crisis management: a named crisis team with pre-assigned decision authority and a documented crisis management plan. BSI IT-Grundschutz DER.4 requires this plan - and of its sixteen requirements, nine explicitly name the management body. The plan must be exercised at minimum annually.

## What happens if you don't have it

The CEO-specific obligations are: approving the crisis management plan, participating in the annual exercise, and being named in the plan as the person who declares a crisis and authorises emergency spending. If you have never attended a crisis exercise, that is itself an audit finding under DER.4. Lesson 2.7 covered the incident response plan - the crisis management plan is the escalation above it, for when the incident is too large for the normal response process.

## What to ask your team

Three questions. "Show me the last backup restore test report - which system, when, how long did it take, and did it work?" "What is our RTO on the core [[business continuity (BCM)|business system]], and what does the last disaster recovery test say the actual recovery time was?" "When is the next crisis exercise, and am I in the calendar for it?"

If any of those answers is missing or vague, that is your first action item.

## Key takeaways

1. Business continuity under Article 21(2)(c) has three parts - backup, disaster recovery, and crisis management - and the Maersk case shows what happens when luck is the only thing between your company and a months-long outage.
2. Untested backups do not count - ask for the restore test report regularly, and if it does not exist, the backup programme is not running.
3. You personally must attend a crisis exercise - BSI DER.4 names the management body in nine of sixteen requirements, and your absence is an audit finding.
