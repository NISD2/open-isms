# Lesson 5.2: Certificate of Completion

You have reached the end of the course. Over the previous four modules, you have learned what the NIS2 Directive requires from you personally, what the ten mandatory measures mean at the management level, how to make the decisions that only you can make, and how to protect yourself when things go wrong.

## Your certificate

When you complete the course, you receive a Certificate of Completion. The certificate states your name (or your verified email address), the course title, the legal basis (Section 38(3) BSIG and Article 20(2)), the completion date, the course duration, the issuer, and in an appendix every lesson covered. It is a participation certificate - it documents that you completed the training, not that you passed an exam.

The certificate carries a clear statement that it documents course completion. It is not a legal certification that you have "sufficient knowledge" in the meaning of Article 20(2). That judgment is made by your regulator, based on your knowledge and your company's actual compliance - not by any training provider. The certificate is your [[training evidence]]. If an auditor asks how you have met the Article 20(2) training duty, the certificate is the first piece of paper you put in front of them.

In its curriculum for management training (October 2026), the BSI lists the minimum content of a training record: date, time and duration, the provider, the participants with their role or function, the content covered, and the reference to Section 38(3) BSIG. The certificate covers provider, date, course duration, content and legal basis. Your role in the company and a participant list of all managing directors, signed or confirmed digitally, you add in your own file.

File the certificate in your company's [[compliance documentation]] - in the same folder as your signed security policy, your risk register, and your incident response plan. Those four documents together are the backbone of your audit file.

## Three actions for the next two weeks

First: schedule a meeting with your CISO inside the next two weeks. Bring the list of the ten measures from Module 2. Walk through them one by one. Ask two questions per measure: what do we have in place, and can you show me the evidence? That meeting generates the current measures [[inventory]] - one of the three artefacts that carry the most weight in an audit.

Second: find your current signed security policy - the top-level document - and read it carefully. Check the date. Check whose name is on it. Check whether the risk analysis referenced in it exists and is current. If any of those three checks fails, put a [[re-sign-off]] on your calendar within the next month.

Third: look at the organisational chart and trace the reporting line from your CISO to you. If there is anyone between the CISO and the management body, start the conversation about restructuring that line. The EU implementing regulation requires direct reporting for the digital providers it covers (CIR Annex 1.2.3) and is the benchmark for everyone else, and the longer you wait, the harder it is to fix.

## Re-training

The directive requires the management body to follow training and sets no fixed [[intervals]]. In Germany, Section 38(3) BSIG requires management to attend training regularly, yet neither the law nor the BSI sets a fixed interval; the BSI recommends choosing duration and interval by the company's risk exposure and the individual skills of its management. Use those criteria to decide how often you schedule a full [[retake|re-take]] of a course like this one.

The law does not reward good intentions. It rewards the paper trail - the signed policy, the dated risk register, the documented sign-off, the filed incident report, the recorded training. Everything in this course was built so that you can produce those [[artefact|artefacts]] and defend them.

## Key takeaways

1. The Certificate of Completion is training evidence for the auditor - it documents your participation, the content covered, and the course duration. Your role and the signed participant list you add yourself.
2. File the certificate alongside your signed security policy, risk register, and incident response plan - those four documents are the backbone of your audit file.
3. Three actions in the next two weeks: meet your CISO and walk the ten measures, read and verify your signed security policy, and trace the CISO reporting line on the organisational chart.
