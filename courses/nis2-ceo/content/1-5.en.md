# Lesson 1.5: Your Duty to Approve and Oversee

Most CEOs assume approving the cybersecurity plan means reading a summary slide and nodding. Under Article 20(1) of the NIS2 Directive, that is the exact behaviour the law is designed to catch and punish. This single sentence creates three separate personal duties - and most leadership teams only notice the first.

## What the rule means

Article 20(1) of the NIS2 Directive states: "Member States shall ensure that the management bodies of essential and important entities approve the cybersecurity risk-management measures taken by those entities in order to comply with Article 21, oversee its implementation and can be held liable for infringements by the entities of that Article."

Three duties, each failing independently. Approve - a [[formal legal act]]. It must be documented, it must name what you are approving, and it must be dated with your personal signature. Oversee - an ongoing obligation that does not end when you sign, requiring regular [[reporting]] from your security team and at least one formal [[annual review]]. Be liable - if the measures fail and the company is fined or suffers a loss, you personally can be the one who pays. Lesson 1.7 covers the liability chain in detail.

The duty applies to all members of the [[management body]], not just the CEO. The duty cannot be delegated. The CISO can implement the measures. Only the management body can approve them.

## Why you care

There is one more requirement most leadership teams miss. You are expected to personally use the [[controls]] you approved. If you approved [[multi-factor authentication]] for staff logins, you use multi-factor authentication. If you approved the training programme, you attend the training. A CEO who exempts themselves from the controls they approved is in direct violation of the [[leading by example]] principle - and an auditor can cite it as a [[finding]].

## What to ask before your next sign-off

Three checks before you sign anything. First: does the document name what you are approving, with a date and your personal signature? Second: is there a process in place for regular reporting back to you on whether the measure is actually being implemented? Third: are you personally using the controls described in the document? If any answer is no, the [[sign-off]] is incomplete as a legal act.

## Key takeaways

1. Article 20(1) creates three personal duties - approve, oversee, and be liable - and each one can fail independently of the others.
2. Approval is a formal legal act: your name and personal signature must appear on the document, and the top-level security policy must be reviewed at minimum every two years.
3. You must personally follow the controls you approved - exempting yourself from multi-factor authentication, training, or any other measure is an audit finding.
