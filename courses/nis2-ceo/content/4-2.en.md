# Lesson 4.2: D&O Insurance - What It Covers and the Cyber Exclusions

Your board secretary forwards the [[renewal]] quote for the company's [[directors and officers liability insurance|directors and officers insurance]]. You sign it and file it without reading the exclusions. The question is not what you paid for that policy - it is whether it pays out when a cyber claim lands on your desk personally. D&O insurance was built to protect directors from business judgment claims, but NIS2 violations are [[statutory duty]] breaches, and most D&O policies either exclude them outright or narrow coverage so tightly that what you think you have is not there when you need it.

## What D&O insurance was built for

Article 20(1) of the NIS2 Directive makes the management body personally responsible for approving and overseeing the cybersecurity measures. Article 32(5) gives regulators the power to impose personal [[sanction|sanctions]] on natural persons exercising managerial functions when there are serious breaches. The law now puts a line on your personal balance sheet that did not exist before.

Directors and officers liability insurance was designed to protect directors from claims of mismanagement - specifically, the allegation that a director made a bad business decision. The policy pays for legal defence costs, [[settlement|settlements]], and court-ordered damages. It pays when you can show you made a reasonable decision with the information you had - the protection it was built for.

## Why NIS2 claims sit in the wrong category

NIS2 violations are not judgment calls. They are statutory duty breaches. The law tells you what to do, you do not do it, and the regulator holds you personally responsible - a different legal category that sits on the wrong side of the line most D&O policies draw.

The most critical clause to check is the cyber liability [[exclusion]]. Many D&O policies now carry it - sometimes labelled explicitly, sometimes buried in broader language. If it is present, your D&O does not cover NIS2 claims against you personally. Lesson 4.4 covers the full list of specific exclusions to read for in your D&O and cyber policies. The [[coverage gap|gap between what you assumed the policy covered and what it actually covers]] is the space where the expensive surprise lives.

## The three questions to ask your [[broker]]

Ask three questions before you sign the next renewal. First: "Does our current D&O policy contain a cyber liability exclusion, in either explicit or implicit form?" If yes, ask for the exact wording. Second: "If I am personally named in a claim by the regulator under Article 32(5) of the NIS2 Directive, does this policy pay for my legal defence?" Anything vague means no. Third: "What does the policy say about statutory duty breaches - not business judgment claims, statutory ones?" If your broker does not know the answer, their next call is to the insurer, and you get the answer in writing.

Get all three answers in writing, dated, and attached to the policy. If you skip these questions and your renewal auto-signs, the first time you find out about the cyber exclusion is the day the regulator files a claim.

## Key takeaways

1. D&O insurance was built for business judgment claims - NIS2 violations are statutory duty breaches, which sit in a different legal category.
2. Many D&O policies now carry an explicit cyber liability exclusion - if your policy has that clause, your D&O does not cover NIS2 claims against you personally.
3. Ask three questions to your broker before every renewal, and get the answers in writing.
