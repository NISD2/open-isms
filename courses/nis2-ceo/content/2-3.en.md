# Lesson 2.3: Risk Treatment Options and Risk Appetite

Every risk on your register ends with one of four decisions - and one of them requires your personal signature on a document the CIR mandates by name: the signed residual risk acceptance. This lesson covers the four options, which ones are off the table for NIS2 entities, and the specific signed document you must produce for every risk you choose to live with.

## What the rule means

Once a risk is identified and scored on an asset (Lesson 2.1, 2.2), the question is what to do with it. There are four options, and only four. Avoid: stop doing the activity that creates the risk. Mitigate: apply controls to reduce the likelihood or impact. Transfer: shift part of the financial consequence to a third party like an insurer. Accept: acknowledge the risk and do nothing more.

For a company in scope of NIS2, transfer and accept are rarely defensible for serious risks. Germany's BSI puts it even more bluntly in its official guidance: "A blanket risk transfer or general risk acceptance is therefore excluded." So you cannot buy a cyber-insurance policy and call compliance closed, and you cannot blanket-state "we accept the residual risk" and close the topic either. The regulator does not care that you are insured. They care whether the service is running and the controls are in place. Lesson 4.1 covers why insurance is not a substitute for controls. In practice, mitigate is the option you will use for nearly every risk in the top-right corner of the matrix.

## Why you care

Whatever is left over after you have applied your controls is called [[residual risk]]. The EU Implementing Regulation - CIR 2024/2690, Annex point 2.1.1 - is direct: "Risk assessment results and residual risks shall be accepted by management bodies." That acceptance is a dated signature on the register or on a separate acceptance sheet, next to each accepted residual risk. The CIR anchors the entire risk-management chain on this signature: if you cannot produce it when the auditor asks, the rest of your risk management is treated as unapproved.

One more thing the CEO owns: the [[risk appetite]] - the level of risk the company is willing to live with overall. You set that boundary, not your CISO. Without it, every [[risk treatment]] decision becomes an argument rather than a process.

## What to ask your team

Two questions. First: "Show me the signed [[residual risk acceptance|residual risk acceptance]]. Is my name on it, and is the date current?" Second: "Do we have a written risk appetite, and does the CISO know what it is?" If either answer is no, those are the first two action items.

## Key takeaways

1. Four options for every risk - avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept - and for NIS2 entities, mitigate is the workhorse because transfer and accept are rarely defensible for serious risks.
2. CIR 2024/2690 Annex 2.1.1 requires the management body to personally accept each residual risk - without the signed acceptance, the rest of risk management is treated as unapproved.
3. Risk appetite is the CEO's boundary, not the CISO's - it must be written down so the CISO can operate within it.
