# Lesson 3.2: Sign-Off in Practice

A stack of paper lands on your desk. Your CISO says: "just sign the bottom page, it is the same as last year." The law says the signature you put on that page is the single thing the regulator will check first. You have four questions to ask before you pick up the pen.

## What sign-off actually is

Article 20(1) of the NIS2 Directive states: "Member States shall ensure that the management bodies of essential and important entities approve the cybersecurity risk-management measures... and oversee its implementation." That is a legal act, not a courtesy. The German auditor standard, based on IT-Grundschutz module ISMS.1.M3, is explicit: the top-level [[information security policy]] must be personally signed by the management body and published in the CEO's name - your name on the front page, your signature at the bottom, the date you signed it visible to any [[auditor|audit]]or who opens the file. Your signature certifies four things: that you reviewed the [[risk analysis]], that the measures address those risks, that the budget to implement them is approved, and that named people are [[accountable]] for carrying them out. The management body decides what and why. The CISO decides how.

## What happens if you sign blind

An uninformed signature is legally [[void]] in most EU jurisdictions. "[[Due care]]" - the universal legal phrase for having done your homework - means you read what you signed and understood enough to know whether it was reasonable. If your team handed you something and said "just sign, it is routine," your signature does not protect them and it does not protect you. If you signed without reviewing and the company has an incident, your signature becomes the evidence that you approved something you did not read - and that evidence works against you in court, not for you.

## What to ask your team

Ask four questions before you sign. First: "Show me the risk analysis this policy refers to - when was it last updated?" If there is no current risk analysis behind the policy, the policy has nothing to stand on. Second: "What has changed since the last [[version]] I signed?" Third: "Has the budget to implement these measures been approved?" A policy that promises controls the company has not funded is a policy the regulator will use against you. Fourth: "Who are the named people responsible for each measure, and do they know they are named?" Unassigned measures are not measures - they are intentions.

## Key takeaways

1. The top-level security policy must carry your personal signature and be published in your name - not the IT department's, not the compliance officer's.
2. Before you sign, ask four questions: where is the risk analysis, what has changed, is the budget approved, and who are the named people accountable.
3. A signature you cannot defend as informed is legally void - and in court it works against you, not for you.
