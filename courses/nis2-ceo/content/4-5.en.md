# Lesson 4.5: First 48 Hours of Crisis Communication

Your systems went down overnight. A journalist has called twice. In the first forty-eight hours, four audiences need different messages in a specific order. The mistakes that destroy companies happen in the first six hours - before the facts are clear but after the pressure to speak becomes unbearable.

## The legal framework for crisis communication

Article 23 of the NIS2 Directive requires notification to your national [[CSIRT]] of any significant incident without undue delay. Article 36 requires notification to affected service recipients where the incident may adversely affect them. Three audiences are legally mandated: the regulator within twenty-four hours, affected customers without undue delay, and the public if the incident is already in the public domain. Three rules apply to all of them: communicate proactively, never speculate, never lie.

## Walking through the Maersk case

On 27 June 2017, Maersk - handling roughly twenty percent of global container shipping - was hit by the NotPetya malware. The company went offline. Total damage: approximately three hundred million euros.

Six decisions defined their response.

**First: designate one [[spokesperson]] in the first hour.** Maersk appointed a single named person for all external communication. Without one voice, managers in different countries give contradictory statements to local media. One voice. One message. One update timeline.

**Second: issue a [[holding statement]] within two hours.** A holding statement acknowledges disruption without speculating on cause or scope, and commits to a specific update time. Silence in the first two hours is when journalists write the story for you from fragments.

**Third: internal before external.** The crisis team and key staff received a one-page briefing on what was known, what was not, and what they were authorized to say. This prevented employees from posting [[speculation]] on social media before the spokesperson had spoken.

**Fourth: file the early warning inside twenty-four hours.** The regulator notification is separate from the press statement. File what you know, mark what you do not, commit to updating within seventy-two hours.

**Fifth: notify affected customers.** Customer notification under Article 36 is separate from both the regulator report and the press statement. Customers who do not hear from you will hear from journalists.

**Sixth: issue the public statement last.** A full statement on the nature, scope, and response comes only after the internal briefing, regulator report, and customer notification. The CEO is typically not the spokesperson on television but is always the final approver of every external statement. Every statement passes [[legal review]] before release.

Maersk communicated the operational reality quickly and honestly, did not speculate on [[attribution]], and used manual processes to keep cargo moving. Analysts rated the handling as a reference case. The entry vector - a compromised software update - was preventable, a valid criticism of the security posture but not of the communication. The three hundred million euros in damage did not cost them trust. The communication discipline preserved it.

## The four audiences in order

Internal first, regulator second, customers third, public fourth. Mixing the sequence creates legal exposure and a [[narrative]] you do not control.

## Key takeaways

1. Four audiences, in order: internal first, regulator second, customers third, public fourth - each with different content and different deadlines.
2. A holding statement inside two hours buys time to get the facts right. Silence in the first two hours is when the story is written for you.
3. The CEO is rarely the spokesperson on television but is always the final approver of every external statement - legal review is not optional.
