# Lesson 4.5: First 48 Hours of Crisis Communication

Your systems went down overnight. A journalist has called twice. In the first forty-eight hours, four audiences need different messages in a specific order. The mistakes that destroy companies happen in the first six hours - before the facts are clear but after the pressure to speak becomes unbearable.

## The legal framework for crisis communication

Article 23 of the NIS2 Directive requires notification to your national [[CSIRT]] of any significant incident without undue delay. Article 23(1) asks you, where appropriate, to notify affected service recipients without undue delay, and Article 23(7) lets the authority require you to inform the public. Three audiences can be legally mandated: the regulator within twenty-four hours, affected customers without undue delay, and the public where the authority requires it. Three rules apply to all of them: communicate proactively, never speculate, never lie.

## Walking through the Maersk case

On 27 June 2017, Maersk - handling roughly twenty percent of global container shipping - was hit by the NotPetya malware. The company went offline. Maersk put the cost at 200 to 300 million US dollars in its interim report of August 2017.

Maersk is often cited as a case of open communication about an attack. The six decisions below are this course's playbook for a response like that; they are not a record of what happened inside Maersk.

**First: designate one [[spokesperson]] in the first hour.** One named person speaks for the company on everything external. Without one voice, managers in different countries give contradictory statements to local media. One voice. One message. One update timeline.

**Second: issue a [[holding statement]] within two hours.** A holding statement acknowledges disruption without speculating on cause or scope, and commits to a specific update time. Silence in the first two hours is when journalists write the story for you from fragments.

**Third: internal before external.** The crisis team and key staff get a one-page briefing on what is known, what is not, and what they are authorised to say. This keeps employees from posting [[speculation]] on social media before the spokesperson has spoken.

**Fourth: file the early warning inside twenty-four hours.** The regulator notification is separate from the press statement. File what you know, mark what you do not, commit to updating within seventy-two hours.

**Fifth: notify affected customers.** Customer notification under Article 23(1) is separate from both the regulator report and the press statement. Customers who do not hear from you will hear from journalists.

**Sixth: issue the public statement last.** A full statement on the nature, scope, and response comes only after the internal briefing, regulator report, and customer notification. The CEO is typically not the spokesperson on television but is always the final approver of every external statement. Every statement passes [[legal review]] before release.

Maersk published a cost estimate for the attack within weeks. The entry vector - a compromised software update - was preventable, a valid criticism of the security posture but not of the communication. What the case gives you for your own plan: decide who speaks, say what you know, say what you do not know yet, and say when you will say more.

## The four audiences in order

Internal first, regulator second, customers third, public fourth. Mixing the sequence creates legal exposure and a [[narrative]] you do not control.

## Key takeaways

1. Four audiences, in order: internal first, regulator second, customers third, public fourth - each with different content and different deadlines.
2. A holding statement inside two hours buys time to get the facts right. Silence in the first two hours is when the story is written for you.
3. The CEO is rarely the spokesperson on television but is always the final approver of every external statement - legal review is not optional.
