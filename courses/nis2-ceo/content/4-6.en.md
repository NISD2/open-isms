# Lesson 4.6: Ransomware Payment Decisions

On 7 May 2021, Colonial Pipeline paid DarkSide about 4.4 million dollars (75 bitcoin) in [[cryptocurrency]] for a [[decryption]] key. The key was so slow they restored from backups anyway. The CEO decided in hours, without a [[pre-agreed policy]].

## The three legal constraints on payment

No EU-wide criminal prohibition on paying a ransom exists. But three legal constraints bite, and every CEO must know them before the pressure arrives.

**First: [[sanctions law]].** EU and US sanctions lists include some [[ransomware]] groups and countries from which they operate. Under US sanctions rules (OFAC), paying a sanctioned group can be a violation even if you did not know. Under the EU regulation on restrictive measures against cyber-attacks, you escape liability only if you did not know and had no reasonable cause to suspect that you were breaching the measures (Council Regulation (EU) 2019/796, Article 10(2)). [[Attribution]] is often unclear in the first hours, so you cannot confirm who you are paying. The sanctions risk is present by default.

**Second: corporate law.** In Germany, [[breach of trust]] under section 266 of the criminal code applies when company money is spent against the company's interest. A ransom where the decryption tool fails, backups were viable, or the attacker disappears falls into that category. The CEO can be personally liable.

**Third: sector-specific rules.** Financial services, critical infrastructure, and defence have additional restrictions. If your company operates in one of these sectors, the restrictions stack.

The principle: three legal tripwires. Never decide payment in the first hours without legal review.

## Walking through the Colonial Pipeline case

Colonial Pipeline operates the largest refined-petroleum pipeline in the United States, Texas to New York. On 7 May 2021, the company detected DarkSide ransomware and shut down operations. Fuel shortages hit the east coast within days.

Within one day, the CEO paid about 4.4 million dollars in Bitcoin. The decryption tool was so slow the company restored from backups anyway. The payment did not save time. It funded the attackers and marked Colonial as a paying target. Law enforcement later recovered a portion.

The [[sanctions list|sanctions]] question was live throughout. DarkSide was believed to be Russia-based and had targeted critical infrastructure before. The CEO told the US Senate that he made the decision to pay himself and called it the hardest decision of his career. It was made within a day, mid-crisis - exactly when it cannot be made well. (The US sanctions authority is [[OFAC]].)

What went right: law enforcement engaged, legal advice obtained, transparency afterwards. What went wrong, as this course reads the case: the decision had to be made from scratch under pressure, the payment did not accelerate recovery, and it marked a critical infrastructure target as willing to pay. A position agreed before the attack closes that gap.

## How to prepare before the attack

Your [[incident response plan]] needs a pre-agreed position on ransomware payment, signed by the CEO. It does not have to say "never pay." It can say "pay only after legal review confirms no sanctions risk, forensics confirms backups are not viable, and the board has been consulted." The decision under pressure becomes a check against policy, not an improvisation.

Four steps before any payment: CEO approval ([[non-delegable]]), legal review covering sanctions and criminal law, law enforcement coordination, and documentation in the incident record.

## Key takeaways

1. Paying does not guarantee recovery - a decryption tool can be slow or fail, and a clean restore from backups is often faster, as it was at Colonial Pipeline.
2. US sanctions law applies regardless of intent; under EU law you escape liability only if you had no reasonable cause to suspect a breach (Council Regulation (EU) 2019/796, Article 10(2)). You cannot always identify the recipient, so the risk is real.
3. Make the payment decision before the attack - a pre-agreed position signed by the CEO turns a crisis improvisation into a policy check.
