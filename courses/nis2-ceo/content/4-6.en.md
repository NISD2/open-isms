# Lesson 4.6: Ransomware Payment Decisions

On 7 May 2021, Colonial Pipeline paid DarkSide approximately four and a half million dollars in [[cryptocurrency]] for a [[decryption]] key. The key was so slow they restored from backups anyway. The CEO decided in hours, without a [[pre-agreed policy]].

## The three legal constraints on payment

No EU-wide criminal prohibition on paying a ransom exists. But three legal constraints bite, and every CEO must know them before the pressure arrives.

**First: [[sanctions law]].** EU and US sanctions lists include some [[ransomware]] groups and countries from which they operate. Paying a sanctioned group is a violation even if you did not know. [[Attribution]] is often unclear in the first hours, so you cannot confirm who you are paying. The sanctions risk is present by default.

**Second: corporate law.** In Germany, [[breach of trust]] under section 266 of the criminal code applies when company money is spent against the company's interest. A ransom where the decryption tool fails, backups were viable, or the attacker disappears falls into that category. The CEO can be personally liable.

**Third: sector-specific rules.** Financial services, critical infrastructure, and defence have additional restrictions. If your company operates in one of these sectors, the restrictions stack.

The principle: three legal tripwires. Never decide payment in the first hours without legal review.

## Walking through the Colonial Pipeline case

Colonial Pipeline operates the largest refined-petroleum pipeline in the United States, Texas to New York. On 7 May 2021, the company detected DarkSide ransomware and shut down operations. Fuel shortages hit the east coast within days.

Within one day, the CEO paid approximately four and a half million dollars in Bitcoin. The decryption tool was so slow the company restored from backups anyway. The payment did not save time. It funded the attackers and marked Colonial as a paying target. Law enforcement later recovered a portion.

The [[sanctions list|sanctions]] question was live throughout. DarkSide was believed to be Russia-based and had targeted critical infrastructure before. The [[OFAC]] analysis ran in parallel with the payment decision - not before it. Treasury later confirmed the risk but did not prosecute.

In [[congressional]] testimony, the CEO stated a pre-agreed policy would have changed the decision. The company had no board-approved position on ransomware payment before the attack. The decision was made mid-crisis - exactly when it cannot be made well.

What went right: law enforcement engaged, legal advice obtained, transparency afterwards. What went wrong: no pre-agreed policy, payment did not accelerate recovery, and it proved critical infrastructure targets will pay. The absence of a pre-agreed policy is itself an audit finding under NIS2.

## How to prepare before the attack

Your [[incident response plan]] needs a pre-agreed position on ransomware payment, signed by the CEO. It does not have to say "never pay." It can say "pay only after legal review confirms no sanctions risk, forensics confirms backups are not viable, and the board has been consulted." The decision under pressure becomes a check against policy, not an improvisation.

Four steps before any payment: CEO approval ([[non-delegable]]), legal review covering sanctions and criminal law, law enforcement coordination, and documentation in the incident record.

## Key takeaways

1. Paying does not guarantee recovery - roughly sixty percent of payments yield usable decryption, often slower than a clean restore from backups.
2. Sanctions law bites regardless of intent - you cannot always identify the recipient, and paying a sanctioned group is illegal even unknowingly.
3. Make the payment decision before the attack - a pre-agreed position signed by the CEO turns a crisis improvisation into a policy check.
