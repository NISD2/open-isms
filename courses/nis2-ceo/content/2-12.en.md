# Lesson 2.12: Measure 7 - Cyber Hygiene and Training

How many [[phishing simulation|phishing simulations]] has your own company sent to you, personally, in the last twelve months? Not to your team. To you. If the answer is "I am too busy for that kind of thing," you have just named an audit finding out loud.

## What this measure means

Article 21(2)(g) of the NIS2 Directive requires "basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training." This is a company-wide measure - it applies to every employee, not just IT. Your obligation as CEO is to make sure the programme exists and covers everyone, including yourself (Article 20(2) adds a separate personal training duty on the management body, covered in Lesson 1.6). Two halves in one measure:

[[Cyber hygiene]] is the short list of daily practices that stop the majority of common attacks - strong passwords, two-factor authentication, regular updates, backups, and cautious behaviour around links and attachments. These are not optional recommendations. They are the baseline the auditor checks against.

Cybersecurity training is the scheduled programme that keeps those practices alive. It applies to everyone - annual awareness training for all staff, [[role-specific training]] for technical and high-risk roles (developers, finance, HR), and a separate programme for the management body. One generic e-learning course for the whole company does not satisfy this requirement. Phishing simulations - periodic fake phishing emails sent to staff - are part of the training programme, with follow-up training for anyone who clicks.  These can be provided internally or externally by third parties.

## What happens if you don't have it

The directive does not put the management body above the training list. It puts you inside it. Article 20(2) personally requires every member of the management body to undergo cybersecurity training (Lesson 1.6 covers the training duty in full). BSI Standard 200-1 Principle 6 goes further: the management body must follow all prescribed security rules themselves, attend training themselves, and lead by example. Lesson 2.15 covers the lead-by-example principle in the context of MFA. Here the point is simpler: a CEO who skips the phishing simulation or exempts themselves from the awareness programme has created a personal audit finding.

## What to ask your team

Two questions. "What percentage of employees completed the mandatory awareness training last year, and how many failed the last phishing simulation?" Those two numbers tell you the state of this measure. "Am I on the mailing list for the phishing simulations, and have I taken the awareness training myself this year?" If the answer to either is no, the action is on you - personally, this month.

## Key takeaways

1. Cyber hygiene is the daily basics; training is the scheduled programme - Article 21(2)(g) requires both, and one course for everyone is not enough.
2. Training applies to all staff (annual awareness), technical roles (role-specific), and the management body (its own programme under Article 20(2)).
3. A CEO who skips the phishing simulation or exempts themselves from training has created a personal audit finding under BSI Standard 200-1 Principle 6.
