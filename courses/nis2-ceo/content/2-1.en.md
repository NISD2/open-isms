# Lesson 2.1: Risk = Likelihood x Impact

Your CISO walks into your office and asks for four hundred thousand euros of new monitoring. Your CFO says no. Under NIS2, that argument is no longer settled by who shouts louder. It is settled by two questions: how likely is the bad thing, and how bad would it be. After this lesson you will be able to look at a [[risk matrix]], find the corner that matters, and know where the budget conversation starts.

## What the rule means

Article 21(1) of the NIS2 Directive requires you to take "appropriate and proportionate" measures to manage cybersecurity risks. How much you spend on security has to match how exposed you are. That exposure is measured with two numbers: [[likelihood]] - how often a bad event might happen - and [[impact]] - how much it would cost you if it did, measured in money, downtime, customers, or harm.

Your CISO will show you a grid called a risk matrix, with likelihood on one axis and impact on the other. Every identified risk gets plotted on it. The risks in the top-right corner - where "very likely" meets "very serious" - are where you have to act first. That corner is where the budget conversation starts and where supervision focuses hardest.

## Why you care

If you sign off on a set of cybersecurity measures without understanding the risk picture behind them, your signature is uninformed - and an uninformed sign-off does not protect you under Article 20(1). The risk matrix is the document that turns "I approved the budget" into "I understood what I was approving." Without it, the sign-off is decorative.

The full list of scored risks sits in a document your CISO calls a [[risk register]]. That register is what makes the budget debate a decision and not an opinion. The next ten lessons cover the ten controls NIS2 requires you to have in place. Each one exists to move a risk out of that top-right corner.

## What to ask your team

At your next security meeting, one question: "Show me the risk matrix and walk me through the top-right corner." If your CISO cannot produce a current matrix with scored risks, the [[risk assessment]] either does not exist or has not been completed. If the matrix exists but the top-right corner has not been discussed at management level, the assessment is not doing its job.

## Key takeaways

1. Every risk decision under NIS2 comes down to two questions - how likely and how bad - and Article 21(1) makes your response to those answers a legal requirement.
2. The top-right corner of the matrix is where the management body's job begins, because that is where the company faces the most exposure.
3. The risk register is the document that turns the budget argument from opinion into evidence - ask to see it, and check whether the dates are current.
