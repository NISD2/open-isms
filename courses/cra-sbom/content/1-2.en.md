# Lesson 1.2: Top-Level vs Transitive Dependencies

Article 13(5) CRA says your SBOM must cover "at least the top-level dependencies." That phrase hides a distinction that matters enormously in practice. This lesson explains the difference between [[top-level dependency|top-level]] and [[transitive dependency|transitive]] dependencies, why the distinction exists in the law, and why best practice diverges from the legal minimum.

## The legal minimum: top-level dependencies

A top-level dependency is a component that your code directly imports, links against, or packages. If your JavaScript project has a `package.json` with `"express": "4.18.2"` listed under `dependencies`, express is a top-level dependency. If your Java project has `<dependency>spring-boot</dependency>` in `pom.xml`, Spring Boot is a top-level dependency.

The CRA minimum covers these. The rationale from the Commission's impact assessment: this is where manufacturers make deliberate choices. You selected express. You selected Spring Boot. You are responsible for those choices.

## What transitive dependencies are

A transitive dependency is a component pulled in by one of your top-level dependencies, not by your code directly. When you install express, npm also installs `accepts`, `array-flatten`, `body-parser`, `content-disposition`, and dozens more. You did not ask for them. Express needs them.

In a modern software project, the ratio of transitive to top-level dependencies is typically 10:1 or higher. A project with 30 direct dependencies may have 300+ transitive ones. You cannot see them by reading your own manifest files.

## Why the CRA stops at top-level

The Commission's 2023 impact assessment acknowledged the practical difficulty of requiring full transitive dependency coverage for all manufacturers. Many small manufacturers do not have the tooling or expertise to enumerate every transitive component. The minimum was set at top-level to make compliance achievable while still covering the majority of supply chain risk at the point of deliberate choice.

## Why best practice covers transitive dependencies

Log4Shell (CVE-2021-44228), published in December 2021, was a transitive dependency in thousands of applications. Teams that had never directly imported `log4j-core` were vulnerable because one of their libraries had imported it, and that library was also a dependency of another library, nested three or four levels deep.

When the vulnerability was announced, the first question was: "Are we affected?" For teams with only top-level SBOMs, the answer required manual investigation across the entire dependency tree. For teams with full transitive SBOMs, a single scanner run answered the question in under a minute.

The [[VEX]] (Vulnerability Exploitability eXchange) statement, a CycloneDX feature, lets you attach a formal "not affected" declaration to a CVE, with a justification. If your SBOM does not include the transitive component at all, you cannot attach a VEX statement to it. The market surveillance authority reviewing your Article 14 compliance has no evidence that you even checked.

## What "at least top-level" means for your compliance posture

The CRA minimum is defensible. A market surveillance authority auditing your SBOM against Article 13(5) will not reject a top-level-only SBOM on completeness grounds, it meets the legal text.

But "defensible" and "effective" are different things. If an actively exploited vulnerability in a transitive component triggers the Article 14 24-hour reporting obligation, and you missed it because your SBOM did not cover transitive dependencies, you have a reporting failure on top of a security failure.

The practical guidance from ENISA's SBOM good practices document (2023) and the NTIA minimum elements for an SBOM both recommend full transitive coverage as best practice. Most SBOM tooling (Syft, Trivy, cdxgen) produces full transitive output by default. There is no cost argument for stopping at top-level when the tools do not stop there.

## How tooling handles the distinction

When you run Syft against a Docker image, it produces a CycloneDX SBOM that includes every package in the image: operating system packages, language runtime, top-level and transitive application dependencies. It does not require you to configure depth separately.

When you run `cdxgen` against a Node.js project directory, it reads `package-lock.json` or `yarn.lock`, both of which record the full resolved dependency tree including transitive components.

The tools default to full coverage. Your SBOM pipeline should not override that default.

## Key takeaways

1. Top-level dependencies are components your code directly imports. The CRA Article 13(5) minimum covers these.
2. Transitive dependencies are components pulled in by your top-level dependencies, not by your code directly. The CRA minimum does not require them, but best practice does.
3. Log4Shell demonstrated the cost of top-level-only SBOMs: thousands of teams could not determine their exposure without manual investigation across the full dependency tree.
4. Most SBOM tools produce full transitive output by default. Do not configure them to stop at top-level.
5. VEX statements in CycloneDX require the transitive component to be present in the SBOM. Without it, you cannot formally document "not affected" decisions for CVEs in nested dependencies.
