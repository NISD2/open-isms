# Lesson 0.1: What an SBOM Is and Why CRA Made It Mandatory

This course teaches you how to build, maintain, and retain a [[SBOM]] that satisfies the Cyber Resilience Act. It is concise. About fifty minutes in total. Each lesson covers one clear job: the legal requirement, the formats, the tooling, and the monitoring that connects your SBOM to Article 14 vulnerability reporting.

This first lesson answers two questions. What is an SBOM? And why did the CRA make it mandatory?

## What an SBOM is

An SBOM is a list. It names every software component in your product, who made it, what version it is, and how to identify it uniquely. Think of it as the ingredient label on packaged food, but for software.

Before SBOMs became a regulatory topic, most manufacturers could not answer a simple question: "What is in your product?" They knew what they wrote. They did not know what their dependencies brought in. The SolarWinds attack in 2020 and the Log4Shell vulnerability in 2021 showed what that ignorance costs. In both cases, a component deep inside thousands of products created a crisis that teams could not triage quickly because they did not know which products were affected.

An SBOM solves the triage problem. When a new vulnerability is published, you run a scan against your SBOM and get an answer in minutes: affected or not affected.

## What the CRA requires

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, the Cyber Resilience Act, entered into force on 10 December 2024. Its main obligations apply from 11 December 2027. The SBOM obligation sits in Annex I, Part II, point (1), among the manufacturer's vulnerability-handling requirements:

> Manufacturers shall "identify and document vulnerabilities and components contained in products with digital elements, including by drawing up a software bill of materials in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the top-level dependencies of the products."

Three things to notice:

1. **"Commonly used and machine-readable format"**: the regulation names no specific format, but the two that qualify are [[CycloneDX]] and [[SPDX]]. Both are machine-readable. Both are used by hundreds of organisations. The European Commission's guidance documents reference both.

2. **"At the very least the top-level dependencies"**: this is the minimum. Your direct dependencies. Not every nested library. Best practice goes deeper, but the legal floor is top-level.

3. **"Identify and document"**: the SBOM is part of the [[technical documentation]] under Article 31 and Annex VII. You must keep it. A [[market surveillance authority]] can request it. You do not publish it to the world, but you must be able to hand it over promptly.

## Why CRA chose SBOM

The SBOM requirement in Annex I, Part II, point (1) is not standalone. It connects to Article 14, which requires manufacturers to notify [[actively exploited vulnerability|actively exploited vulnerabilities]] to the CSIRT designated as coordinator and to ENISA, with an early warning within 24 hours. You cannot report a vulnerability affecting your product if you do not know what is in your product.

The chain is:

1. SBOM tells you what components your product contains.
2. A vulnerability feed (NVD, GitHub Advisory Database) tells you which components have known CVEs.
3. Your monitoring tool matches SBOM against feed and raises an alert.
4. You assess whether the vulnerability is actually exploitable in your product context.
5. If it is actively exploited, you send an early warning to your coordinating CSIRT and ENISA within 24 hours, followed by a fuller notification within 72 hours.

No SBOM means no monitoring. No monitoring means you cannot meet the Article 14 reporting obligation.

## What counts as a product with digital elements

The CRA applies to any [[product with digital elements]], hardware or software that can connect, directly or indirectly, to another device or network. This is a broad scope. Firmware, consumer apps, industrial controllers, network appliances, operating systems, and enterprise software all fall in.

Open-source software distributed commercially is in scope. Software provided as a service is generally out of scope (that is NIS 2 territory). Pure professional services without a software product are out of scope.

If you are unsure whether your product is in scope, Article 3 CRA and Recitals 9 to 15 give the detailed criteria.

## Key takeaways

1. An SBOM is a machine-readable list of every software component in your product, with names, versions, suppliers, and identifiers.
2. CRA Annex I, Part II, point (1) requires manufacturers of products with digital elements to produce an SBOM in a commonly used, machine-readable format covering at least top-level dependencies.
3. The SBOM is part of the technical documentation under Article 31. Not public, but available to market surveillance authorities on request.
4. The SBOM is the foundation of Article 14 vulnerability reporting. Without it, you cannot monitor for or report actively exploited vulnerabilities.
5. CRA entered into force December 2024. Main obligations apply from December 2027. Vulnerability reporting obligations apply from September 11, 2026.
